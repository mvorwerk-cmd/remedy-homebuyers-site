Inherited a House in Indianapolis? A Plain-English Guide to Indiana Probate

By Matt Vorwerk August 17, 2026 9 min read

Inheriting a house in Indianapolis usually arrives at the worst possible moment. Someone you loved has died, the funeral is barely behind you, and now there is a property in Irvington or Garfield Park with a mortgage, a lawn that needs mowing, and a stack of mail you are not sure you are allowed to open. On top of that, a lawyer has started using words like “letters testamentary” and “unsupervised administration.”

Here is the reassuring part: Indiana’s probate system is one of the more workable ones in the country, and most Marion County estates move faster and cost less than heirs expect. This guide walks through what actually happens, in the order it happens, and what your realistic choices are at the end. It is written to be useful whether you sell the house to us, list it with an agent, or move into it yourself.

Step 1: Find Out Whether the House Has to Go Through Probate at All

Probate is the court process that moves a deceased person’s property to the people who inherit it. Not every Indianapolis house needs it. What matters is how the previous owner held title:

  • A living trust. If the home was properly transferred into a revocable living trust during the owner’s lifetime, it skips probate entirely. The successor trustee named in the trust document can sell the house without asking a judge for permission.
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety. Title passes automatically to the surviving co-owner. For married couples in Indiana, a jointly held home is typically held as tenants by the entirety, which is why a surviving spouse usually has nothing to file at all.
  • A transfer on death (TOD) deed. Indiana’s Transfer on Death Property Act (Indiana Code 32-17-14) lets an owner record a deed naming a beneficiary who takes the property automatically at death. The deed has to be recorded with the county recorder before the owner dies. If one was recorded in Marion County, the named beneficiary owns the house already.
  • Sole ownership, with a will or without one. This is the case that requires probate. The will (if there is one) is filed with the court, a personal representative is appointed, and the estate is administered.

Before you do anything else, pull the deed. Marion County property records are searchable online, and a title company will run it for free if you are considering a sale. Ten minutes of looking can save you nine months of court.

Worth knowing: Indiana’s small estate affidavit under Indiana Code 29-1-8-1 covers estates where the probate assets total $100,000 or less, and it can be used 45 days after the death. It is a genuinely useful shortcut — but it applies to personal property, not real estate. A house almost always still requires an estate to be opened.

Step 2: Understand Supervised vs. Unsupervised Administration

This is the single biggest fork in the road for an Indiana estate, and it determines how long the whole thing takes.

Unsupervised administration

Governed by Indiana Code 29-1-7.5, unsupervised administration lets the personal representative handle the estate — including selling real estate — without returning to the judge for approval at each step. To get it, the estate generally has to be solvent, the personal representative has to be someone the court considers suitable, and the heirs (or the beneficiaries under the will) have to consent in writing. That consent is filed with the court alongside the petition.

Most Marion County estates with cooperating families end up here. The practical difference is enormous: a house can be listed, put under contract, and closed without a court hearing scheduled around a judge’s calendar.

Supervised administration

If an heir will not consent, if there is real conflict about who should serve, or if the estate is insolvent, the court supervises. That means court approval for the sale of the house, formal accountings, and a slower clock. Supervised estates in Marion County commonly run 9 to 18 months; a contested one can run longer.

If you are an heir and someone asks you to sign a consent to unsupervised administration, read it, ask questions, and understand that signing generally speeds things up for everyone — but do not sign it just to keep the peace if you have a real concern about who is handling the money.

Step 3: Respect the Claims Clock

Once an estate is opened, the personal representative publishes a notice to creditors. Under Indiana Code 29-1-14-1, creditors generally have three months from that first published notice to file claims, and all claims subject to that section are barred if they are not filed within nine months of the date of death.

That three-month window is the piece most heirs bump into. You can usually market the house during it, and with unsupervised administration you can often close during it too — but a careful title company or attorney may want the window to run before distributing sale proceeds to heirs, so nobody has to claw money back later. Ask early. It is a normal question and it changes your timeline.

One more item that catches families off guard: Indiana Code 29-1-4-1 gives a surviving spouse a $25,000 allowance from the estate (and, if there is no surviving spouse, the decedent’s children under 18 share it). If the estate’s personal property does not cover it, that allowance can reach the real estate. If there is a surviving spouse and not much cash in the estate, factor this in before anyone counts on a particular payout.

Step 4: The Stepped-Up Basis — The One Piece of Good Tax News

Indiana repealed its inheritance tax effective for deaths after December 31, 2012, so there is no Indiana inheritance tax to worry about today. And federal estate tax only touches estates far larger than a typical Indianapolis home.

The bigger deal is the stepped-up basis. When you inherit real estate, your cost basis resets to the property’s fair market value on the date of the owner’s death. If your father bought the Meridian-Kessler house in 1981 for $48,000 and it was worth $315,000 the day he died, your basis is $315,000. Sell it near that number in the months that follow and you are likely to owe little or no capital gains tax — decades of appreciation simply do not get taxed.

Two practical steps: get a written valuation dated as close to the date of death as possible (a formal appraisal is best, a detailed broker’s opinion is often acceptable), and keep every receipt for cleanout, repairs, and selling costs. Give both to whoever prepares the estate’s tax return. This is a place where an hour of paperwork can be worth thousands of dollars.

Step 5: Know What You Have to Disclose

Indiana normally requires sellers of one-to-four-unit residential property to complete the Seller’s Residential Real Estate Sales Disclosure form under Indiana Code 32-21-5. That chapter, however, does not apply to transfers made in the administration of an estate — which is why a personal representative selling the family home is generally exempt from the standard form.

Exempt from the form is not the same as free to stay quiet about a known problem. If you know the basement takes water every spring or that the furnace failed last winter, say so in writing. Honest disclosure protects you from a lawsuit long after closing, and any buyer worth dealing with would rather hear it upfront. It also costs you less than you would think — cash buyers price in the repairs anyway.

Step 6: Decide What to Do With the House

Four paths, none of them wrong. The right one depends on your money, your time, and how far you live from Indianapolis.

Option A: Keep it and move in

The simplest choice if the house genuinely fits your life. Indiana’s homestead standard deduction and supplemental deduction meaningfully lower the property tax bill on an owner-occupied home, so make sure the homestead deduction is filed with the Marion County Auditor after title changes hands — inherited properties frequently lose it in the shuffle and the owner does not notice until the bill arrives.

Option B: Rent it out

Indianapolis has a real rental market, especially for single-family homes in Fountain Square, Garfield Park, and near Broad Ripple. But landlording is a job. Screening tenants, chasing maintenance, and covering vacancy from another state is rough duty. A rough rule of thumb: if the house needs more than about $15,000 of work before it is rentable, renting is rarely the fastest route to cash.

Option C: Repair it and list it on the market

This produces the highest number on the top line, and for a house in decent shape in a strong pocket like Irvington or Meridian-Kessler, it is often the right call. Budget honestly: $10,000 to $60,000 in repairs and updates, 30 to 90 days on market, roughly 5–6% in commissions, 1–2% in closing costs, and every month of taxes, insurance, utilities, and lawn care until it closes. An empty Indianapolis house typically costs $900 to $1,800 a month to hold.

Option D: Sell as-is to a cash buyer

No repairs, no showings, no financing contingency, and a closing date you pick — often 7 to 21 days once the estate can legally convey. The honest tradeoff is price: a cash offer is generally 70–85% of what the home would fetch fully renovated on the MLS, because the buyer is absorbing the repairs, the holding costs, and the risk. That tradeoff is worth it when speed, certainty, or “I never want to see that basement again” matter more than squeezing out the last dollar. It is not worth it when the house is already market-ready and you have time.

Handling the Contents — and the Family

Most heirs underestimate the stuff. A lifetime of belongings takes days or weeks to sort, and if there is any chance of a will contest, you want a record of what was in the house. Walk every room with your phone’s video camera before anything moves. Check drawers, coat pockets, freezer, and the pages of books for cash, jewelry, deeds, and insurance policies. Estate sale companies in Indianapolis typically take 25–40% of gross proceeds, which pencils out if there are 50 or more items of real value. A full cleanout usually runs $1,500 to $4,500 — though a cash buyer will often take the house with the contents still inside, which quietly removes the whole line item.

If you are not the only heir, that is usually harder than anything the court asks of you. A few ground rules that keep families intact: agree on one independent valuation upfront and accept whatever number it produces; decide early whether you are selling or keeping, because mixed goals almost always end in a forced sale months later at a worse price; put every agreement in writing, even by email; and if one heir wants to buy out the others, use that same valuation plus a modest premium to reflect the convenience of skipping a market sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does probate take in Marion County before I can sell the house?

With unsupervised administration and cooperating heirs, a personal representative can often sign a purchase agreement within a few weeks of being appointed, and close shortly after. Supervised administration adds court approval to the sale and commonly stretches the process to 9 to 18 months. The variable that matters most is not the market — it is which track your estate is on and whether the creditor claim period has run.

Can I sell an inherited Indianapolis house before probate is finished?

Often, yes. Once the court appoints a personal representative and issues letters, that person has authority to convey the property — immediately in an unsupervised estate, or after court approval in a supervised one. What you generally cannot do is sell before anyone has legal authority over the estate. If a buyer tells you paperwork does not matter, that is a reason to walk away.

Do all the heirs have to agree to sell an inherited house in Indiana?

If the estate is being administered and the personal representative has authority to sell, the sale does not require unanimous heir consent — though the heirs’ consent is what makes unsupervised administration possible in the first place. If the house has already been distributed and several heirs own it together, then yes, everyone on title has to sign, and a stalemate can end in a partition action. That is slow and expensive for everyone, which is why agreeing early is worth real money.

What happens to the mortgage on a house I inherited in Indianapolis?

The mortgage does not disappear at death, and the lien stays attached to the property. Federal rules generally protect an heir who inherits a home from having the loan called due simply because of the transfer, and successors in interest have the right to get loan information and apply for assistance. Keep the payments current if you can — a foreclosure clock running alongside a probate clock is the one combination that genuinely costs families their equity.

The Short Version

Check the deed before you assume you need probate. If probate is required, push for unsupervised administration if the family is on the same page. Watch the creditor claim window. Get a date-of-death valuation and protect your stepped-up basis. Then choose between keeping, renting, listing, or selling as-is based on your actual situation — not on which option sounds best in the abstract.

And if you are an out-of-state heir staring at a house you have never lived in, know that you are the most common version of this story, not an unusual one. There is a path forward from here, and it does not have to take a year.
