Florida's New Property Disclosure Rules 2026: What Miami Sellers Must Disclose

By Matt Vorwerk July 13, 2026 8 min read

If you're selling a home in Miami this year, the rules about what you have to tell your buyer have changed — and the biggest change is about water. Florida rewrote its residential flood disclosure requirements in the last two years, and as of 2026 those rules are in full effect for every home sale in Miami-Dade County. Add in the disclosure duties that were already on the books, and it's worth taking a few minutes to understand exactly what you're required to reveal before you sign a contract.

The good news: none of this is complicated once someone explains it in plain English. Disclosure isn't a trap, and it isn't designed to scare buyers away from your house. It's about writing down what you honestly know so the person buying your home isn't blindsided later. This guide walks through the current rules, the new flood law, and what all of it means whether you're selling a condo in Brickell, a bungalow in Coconut Grove, or a family home in Kendall.

The Rule Underneath Everything: Johnson v. Davis

Before we get to the new law, it helps to understand the foundation. Florida's core disclosure duty doesn't come from a statute at all — it comes from a 1985 Florida Supreme Court case called Johnson v. Davis. That decision established a rule that still governs every residential sale in the state: a seller must disclose any facts that materially affect the value of the property and are not readily observable to the buyer.

In plain terms, if you know about a real problem that a buyer walking through the house wouldn't spot on their own, you have to tell them. A roof that leaks in heavy rain, a history of termite damage, a foundation crack behind a bookshelf, a permit that was never closed out — these are the kinds of "material defects" the rule is about.

One point trips people up constantly, so it's worth stating clearly: selling "as-is" does not cancel your duty to disclose. An as-is sale limits the repairs a buyer can demand during the inspection period. It does not give you permission to hide a known defect. You can absolutely sell a Miami house in as-is condition — sellers do it every day — but you still have to be honest about what you know.

The simple test: If you know something about the house that would matter to a reasonable buyer, and they couldn't easily see it themselves during a normal walk-through, write it down and disclose it. When in doubt, disclose.

What's New: Florida's Flood Disclosure Law

Here's the change that matters most for Miami. Florida used to be one of the few coastal states with no statutory requirement to disclose a property's flood history. That gap closed with House Bill 1049, codified as Florida Statute § 689.302, which took effect October 1, 2024, and was expanded effective October 1, 2025.

Under the current version of the law, before or at the time a residential sales contract is signed, the seller must provide a written flood disclosure covering three things:

  • Whether the seller has filed a flood-damage insurance claim on the property (for example, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood policy).
  • Whether the seller has received federal or other assistance for flood damage — the 2025 expansion removed the old requirement that the aid be specifically federal, so any flood-repair assistance now counts.
  • The seller's knowledge of any flooding that damaged the property during the time they owned it — not just flooding that led to an insurance claim.

That last point is the heart of the 2025 expansion. Under the original 2024 law, you only had to speak up if you'd filed a claim. Now, if you know the property took on water and was damaged during your ownership, you disclose it — claim or no claim. For a city like Miami, where king tides, tropical storms, and the occasional stalled downpour are simply part of life, this is a meaningful shift.

If you own rental property, the same three disclosures now apply to leases of one year or longer under a companion statute, Florida Statute § 83.512. That's outside the scope of selling, but worth knowing if you're a Miami landlord thinking about your next steps.

Miami-specific reality: Flood zones vary block by block here. A home in low-lying parts of Little Havana or near the water in Coconut Grove may sit in a very different flood picture than a property up on the ridge in Miami Shores. The disclosure law is about your actual knowledge of flooding — so focus on what has really happened to your home, not on what a flood map predicts.

The Disclosures That Were Already Required

The flood law is new, but it joins a short list of disclosures Florida has required for years. If you're selling in Miami, you'll typically see these in or attached to the contract:

Radon gas notice

Under Florida Statute § 404.056(5), every contract for the sale or lease of a building must include a specific written notice about radon gas — a naturally occurring radioactive gas that can accumulate in buildings. The statute even provides the exact wording. This isn't a claim that your house has a radon problem; it's a standard, state-mandated informational notice that goes in essentially every Florida real estate contract.

Property tax notice

Under Florida Statute § 689.261, the contract must include a disclosure warning the buyer that property taxes may increase substantially after the sale — particularly because the previous owner's homestead exemption and Save Our Homes assessment cap do not transfer to the new owner. In a market like Miami's, where assessed values have climbed for years, a buyer's first tax bill can be a genuine surprise, so this notice matters.

Condo and HOA disclosures

Selling a condo in Brickell or a townhome in a Doral association? Florida law requires you to give buyers governing documents and financial information for the association, and buyers get a statutory window to review them. If your unit is in a building affected by Florida's post-2021 structural reserve and inspection requirements, expect buyers to ask detailed questions about reserves and any special assessments — have those documents ready.

How to Handle Disclosure the Right Way

Disclosure works best when you treat it as simple honesty rather than a legal hurdle. A few practical habits keep Miami sellers out of trouble:

  • Write it down. Verbal disclosures are nearly impossible to prove later. Use a written disclosure form and keep a signed copy. A paper trail protects you as much as the buyer.
  • Disclose what you know, not what you guess. You're not required to hire investigators or become an expert on your own house. The duty is about your actual knowledge. "I don't know" is an honest and acceptable answer when it's true.
  • Don't paper over a problem. Painting over a water stain the week before listing, or steering an inspector away from a room, is exactly the kind of concealment that turns a normal sale into a lawsuit.
  • Keep your records. Insurance claims, repair invoices, permit paperwork, and any assistance you received after a storm all help you disclose accurately — and prove you did.

What happens if a seller hides a known material defect? The buyer can generally sue after closing to rescind the sale or recover damages. That's a slow, expensive, stressful outcome for everyone. Honest disclosure up front is almost always cheaper and cleaner than a fight down the road.

Does Disclosing a Problem Kill Your Sale?

This is the worry we hear most, and the honest answer is no. Disclosing flood history, an old roof, or a past repair doesn't stop a home from selling — it just makes sure the buyer is buying with eyes open. Plenty of Miami buyers are perfectly comfortable purchasing a home that has flooded, especially at a price that reflects the condition.

If your home has a history that makes a traditional, financed sale complicated — flood damage, deferred repairs, an open permit, or a condo caught up in special assessments — a direct cash sale is often the simplest path. A cash buyer purchases the home as-is, factors the known issues into a written offer, and closes without waiting on a lender's appraisal or repair conditions. You still disclose everything; the difference is that the buyer has already accounted for it. That's the model we use at Remedy Home Buyers across Miami, from Wynwood to Kendall.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to fill out a seller's disclosure form in Florida?

Florida doesn't mandate one specific state form, but under Johnson v. Davis you must disclose known material defects that a buyer can't readily see. Most Miami sales use a standard written disclosure form because it's the cleanest way to document that you met your duty — and it protects you if a question comes up later.

Does the new flood disclosure law apply if I'm selling as-is?

Yes. Florida Statute § 689.302 requires the written flood disclosure at or before the contract is signed on residential property, regardless of whether the sale is as-is. As-is limits the repairs a buyer can demand — it does not reduce what you're required to disclose.

What if I genuinely don't know whether my house ever flooded?

The law is tied to your knowledge. If you truly don't know of any flooding during your ownership, you disclose that honestly. Where sellers get into trouble is claiming ignorance about flooding they clearly knew about — for example, damage they filed a claim for or repaired themselves.

Can I still sell a Miami home that has flooded and get a fair price?

Yes. Disclosing flood history doesn't block a sale. Many buyers — including cash buyers like Remedy Home Buyers — purchase flood-affected homes as-is and build the property's real condition into a fair, transparent offer. Disclosure is about honesty, not about scaring buyers off.
