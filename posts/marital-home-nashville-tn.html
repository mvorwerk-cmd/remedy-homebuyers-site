Selling the Marital Home in Nashville: Tennessee Equity Split Rules

By Matt Vorwerk July 20, 2026 8 min read

For most couples, the house is the biggest thing they own together — and in a divorce, it's often the hardest thing to untangle. If you're separating in the Nashville area and trying to figure out what happens to the home, you're probably getting conflicting advice from friends, family, and the internet. This guide lays out how Tennessee actually handles the marital home, in plain English, so you can make a clear-headed decision instead of a stressed one.

A quick note before we start: this is educational information, not legal advice. Every divorce has details that change the answer, and you should run your specific situation past a Tennessee family-law attorney. What follows is the framework most Nashville couples work within.

First: Is the House Even "Marital Property"?

Before anyone can split the home, Tennessee law has to classify it. The state draws a line between two kinds of property:

  • Separate property generally belongs to one spouse alone — things owned before the marriage, or received during it as an individual gift or inheritance.
  • Marital property is generally what the couple acquired or built up during the marriage, and it's what gets divided in the divorce.

A house one spouse bought years before the wedding can start as separate property. But it can become partly or fully marital through what lawyers call "commingling" or "transmutation" — for example, if both spouses paid the mortgage from a joint account, both are on the deed, or marital money funded a major remodel. The equity built up during the marriage is frequently treated as marital even when the original purchase was not. This is one of the most fact-specific questions in any divorce, and it's exactly the kind of thing worth an attorney's eyes.

Tennessee Is an "Equitable Distribution" State — Fair, Not Necessarily Equal

This is the single most important thing to understand, because it surprises a lot of people. Tennessee is not a community-property state. Couples in California or Texas typically split marital assets 50/50 by default. Tennessee doesn't work that way.

Under Tennessee Code Annotated § 36-4-121, a court divides marital property "equitably" — meaning fairly — in whatever proportions the court "deems just." Fair does not automatically mean an even 50/50 cut. A judge can award one spouse 55%, 60%, or more of the marital estate if the circumstances justify it. Notably, the statute directs the court to divide property without regard to marital fault — so who "caused" the divorce generally doesn't shift the property split (though fault can matter for other issues like alimony).

What this means for your house: There's no automatic rule that you each get half the equity. The court weighs a list of factors — and it also has specific authority under § 36-4-121 to award the family home, or the right to live in it for a reasonable period, with special consideration for the spouse who has physical custody of the children.

When a judge decides what's fair, § 36-4-121 points to factors that include how long you were married, each spouse's age and health, each spouse's earning capacity and financial needs, the contributions each made to acquiring and preserving the property (including a stay-at-home spouse's contributions as a homemaker), the value of each spouse's separate property, the economic circumstances of each spouse when the division takes effect, and the tax consequences of the split. The takeaway: the more you and your spouse can agree on between yourselves, the less you leave to a judge weighing those factors for you.

The Nashville Divorce Timeline (and Why the House Often Waits)

Most Tennessee divorces are filed on the ground of "irreconcilable differences" — an agreed, no-fault divorce. (The list of fault-based grounds lives in T.C.A. § 36-4-101, but the uncontested path is what most couples use.) That route comes with a mandatory cooling-off period set by T.C.A. § 36-4-103:

  • 60 days from the filing date before the divorce can be finalized if you have no minor children under 18.
  • 90 days from the filing date if you do have a minor child under 18.

No Tennessee judge can waive those minimums. And an irreconcilable-differences divorce can't be finalized until the couple has a written agreement covering property division (and, if there are kids, custody and support). In practice, that means the fate of the marital home usually needs to be settled — on paper — before the divorce is done. Contested cases where the spouses can't agree can stretch well beyond those minimums, often many months, as the court works through the details.

Four Realistic Ways Nashville Couples Handle the Home

Once you know whether the house is marital and roughly how the equity might split, you've got four practical paths. None is automatically "right" — the best one depends on your finances, your timeline, and how amicable things are.

Option A: One spouse keeps the house and buys the other out

If one of you wants to stay — often the parent keeping the kids in their school zone in Green Hills or East Nashville — that spouse can buy out the other's share of the equity. The catch is almost always financing. To pull the departing spouse off the mortgage, the staying spouse usually has to refinance the loan in their name alone, which means qualifying on one income and, in 2026's rate environment, often trading a low pandemic-era mortgage for a higher current rate. Run those new monthly numbers carefully before committing to keep the house.

Option B: Sell on the open market and split the proceeds

Listing with a Nashville agent typically produces the highest sale price, and for couples with a well-kept home and time to spare, it's often the right call. Be realistic about the friction: you'll both need to agree on price, agent, and every offer; keep the home show-ready through weeks of listing; and split not just the equity but the costs — roughly 5–6% in commissions plus 1–2% in closing costs, all deducted before either of you sees a dollar. When two people who are separating have to cooperate on repairs and showings, the process can get tense.

Option C: Keep it (for now) and co-own temporarily

Some couples agree to hold the house for a defined window — until kids finish a school year, or until the market or their finances improve — then sell. This can work, but only with a written agreement spelling out who pays the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and repairs in the meantime, and exactly when and how you'll sell. Vague "we'll figure it out later" arrangements between exes tend to unravel.

Option D: Sell as-is to a direct cash buyer

When speed and certainty matter more than squeezing out the last dollar — a house that needs repairs neither spouse wants to fund, a mortgage that's getting hard to cover on one income, or two people who simply want a clean break — a direct cash sale can make sense. You skip repairs, showings, and financing contingencies; pick a closing date that fits the divorce timeline; and get a single, clear number to divide. The honest trade-off: a cash offer comes in below full retail (generally 70–85% of renovated market value), because the buyer takes on the repairs, costs, and risk. We're transparent about that. If maximum price is your priority and you have the time and cooperation to list, Option B usually nets more.

A clean number to divide: One reason divorcing couples consider a cash sale is simplicity. A firm cash offer with a set closing date turns "the house" from an open-ended argument into a single figure your settlement can be built around — no waiting on a buyer's loan, no renegotiating after an inspection.

Don't Forget the Mortgage, the Deed, and the Equity Math

A divorce decree divides ownership between you and your spouse, but it does not change your contract with the lender. If both names are on the mortgage, both remain legally on the hook until the loan is refinanced or paid off — usually through a sale. A common and costly mistake: one spouse "gets the house" in the settlement but never refinances, leaving the other spouse's credit tied to a loan they no longer control. Settle how and when the mortgage will be resolved, in writing, as part of the divorce.

To estimate the equity you're actually dividing, start with a realistic current value, subtract the mortgage payoff, then subtract the costs of whichever exit you choose (agent commissions and closing costs for a market sale; refinance costs for a buyout). What's left is the number on the table.

A Quick Word on the Nashville Market

Nashville's housing market has stayed comparatively strong, but conditions vary block to block. A turnkey home in Sylvan Park or 12South behaves very differently from a dated house in Antioch, Donelson, or Madison that needs work. If your home is in great shape and you have the time and cooperation to list it, the open market will likely reward you. If it needs significant repairs, or you need a firm closing date to keep the divorce moving, weigh a cash sale against the months and costs a traditional listing can add. Frequently Asked Questions

Do we have to split the house 50/50 in a Tennessee divorce?

No. Tennessee is an equitable-distribution state under T.C.A. § 36-4-121, which means marital property is divided fairly — not automatically equally. A court can award one spouse more than half of the equity if the statutory factors (length of marriage, earning capacity, contributions, and others) justify it. Many couples still agree to a roughly even split, but the law doesn't require one.

Can we sell the house before the divorce is final?

Often, yes — if both spouses agree. Many couples sell during the divorce and hold the net proceeds (sometimes in escrow) until the settlement divides them. Because an irreconcilable-differences divorce requires a written property agreement and a 60- or 90-day waiting period under § 36-4-103, selling early can actually help by turning the home into a clear dollar figure. Just be sure any sale and the division of proceeds are documented in your agreement, and check with your attorney first.

What happens to the house if one of us wants to keep it?

The spouse staying generally buys out the other's share of the equity and refinances the mortgage into their own name to remove the departing spouse from the loan. The hurdle is usually qualifying for that refinance on a single income at current rates. If the numbers don't work, couples often sell instead and split the proceeds.

The house needs repairs and neither of us can afford them. What now?

You have options. You can sell it on the market as-is (expect a lower price and pickier buyers), split the cost of only the highest-return fixes, or sell directly to a cash buyer who takes the home in its current condition. A cash sale won't fetch full retail, but it removes the repair burden entirely and gives you a firm number and closing date to build the settlement around — which many divorcing couples value more than squeezing out the last dollar.
