Foreclosure in Orlando: Florida's Judicial Process and 5 Ways Out

By Matt Vorwerk August 3, 2026 8 min read

If you've fallen behind on your mortgage in Orlando, the first thing worth knowing is this: you almost certainly have more time and more options than the situation feels like it's giving you. Florida's foreclosure process is slower and more structured than most people expect, and at nearly every stage there is a door you can still walk through. This guide explains, in plain English, exactly how foreclosure works in Florida, what the timeline actually looks like, and five realistic ways homeowners in the Orlando area get out from under it.

We've helped families across Orange County — from College Park and Conway to Baldwin Park, Lake Nona, Winter Park, and Pine Hills — work through this exact problem. Some sold to us. Many didn't, and that was the right call for them. Either way, the goal here is to help you understand your position so you can make a calm, informed decision.

First: Being Behind Isn't the Same as Foreclosure

Missing a payment or two puts you in default, not foreclosure. Under federal rules, your loan servicer generally cannot start a foreclosure until you are more than 120 days past due. That 120-day window exists specifically to give you time to apply for help — a loan modification, forbearance, or repayment plan. It's one of the most valuable stretches of time you have, and it's easy to waste it by avoiding the mail.

When the 120 days pass and you still haven't resolved the default, the lender's attorney files a lawsuit. In Florida, that lawsuit is the actual start of foreclosure — and because Florida is a "judicial" state, that word matters a great deal.

How Judicial Foreclosure Works in Florida

Florida is a judicial foreclosure state, governed by Chapter 702 of the Florida Statutes. That means a lender cannot simply schedule an auction and take your home the way they can in states like Georgia or Texas. They have to sue you in county court — for an Orlando property, that's the Ninth Judicial Circuit in Orange County — and a judge has to sign off before your home can be sold. The upside for you is time and a formal chance to be heard. Here's the sequence:

  • Lis pendens and complaint. The lender records a "lis pendens" (a public notice that a lawsuit affecting the property has been filed) and serves you with a foreclosure complaint. This is the official start.
  • Your 20 days to respond. Once you're served, you generally have 20 days to file a written answer with the court. This is the single most important deadline in the whole process. If you ignore it, the lender can ask for a default judgment and things move quickly. If you respond — even with a simple answer — you preserve your right to be heard and slow everything down.
  • Summary judgment. Most Florida foreclosures don't go to a full trial. The lender files a motion for summary judgment, and if the paperwork is in order and you have no valid defense, the judge grants it.
  • Final judgment and sale date. The judge enters a final judgment stating the amount owed and sets a public auction date, usually 20 to 35 days out. In Orange County these sales are conducted online.
  • Certificate of sale and certificate of title. After the auction, the clerk files a certificate of sale. Roughly 10 days later, if no objections are filed, the clerk issues a certificate of title and ownership formally transfers to the winning bidder.

Florida law also gives lenders an expedited path. Under Florida Statutes section 702.10, a lender can ask the court to issue an "order to show cause" — essentially requiring you to explain in a hearing why the foreclosure shouldn't proceed immediately. If you don't respond, the process can move faster than the standard track, which is one more reason not to let deadlines slide.

Reality check: A contested Florida foreclosure often takes 8 to 14 months from the filing of the complaint to the auction, and sometimes longer when courts are backed up. That's real time you can use — to sell, to modify, or to catch up — but only if you stay engaged instead of waiting for it to go away.

Your Right of Redemption

Florida gives you a "right of redemption," which means the right to stop the sale by paying the full amount owed — the judgment balance plus costs — before it's too late. Under Florida Statutes section 45.0315, that right lasts until the later of the clerk filing the certificate of sale or the time specified in the final judgment. Once the certificate of sale is filed, the right of redemption is gone. In practical terms: you can save the home right up to the auction if you can pay it off or refinance, but the window closes fast once the gavel falls.

What About a Deficiency Judgment?

A common fear is owing money even after losing the home. It can happen — it's called a deficiency judgment, and it's the gap between what you owed and what the home sold for at auction. Florida addresses this in Florida Statutes section 702.06. For owner-occupied residential property, the deficiency generally can't exceed the difference between the total judgment amount and the property's fair market value on the date of sale — not simply the (often low) auction price. The lender also has to ask the court for a deficiency judgment within one year of the sale. Knowing this matters, because the fear of a bottomless debt keeps some homeowners frozen when a well-timed sale would have wiped out most or all of the exposure.

Five Realistic Ways Out

No single option is right for everyone. What's right depends on how much equity you have, how much time is left on the clock, whether you want to keep the house, and how much stress you can carry right now.

1. Reinstate or pay off the loan

If your hardship was temporary — a job gap that's now filled, a medical bill that's behind you — you may be able to "reinstate" by paying all missed payments, late fees, and the lender's costs in one lump sum. Because of Florida's right of redemption, you can do this right up until the certificate of sale is filed. If you have significant equity, a refinance or a hard-money bridge loan can also pay the mortgage off entirely and reset the clock.

2. Loan modification, forbearance, or repayment plan

Your servicer would usually rather keep you paying than foreclose. A loan modification permanently changes your terms — lowering the rate, extending the term, or rolling the past-due amount into the balance. Forbearance pauses or reduces payments temporarily. A repayment plan spreads the arrears over several months on top of your normal payment. Apply early, in writing, and keep copies of everything. This is the path that keeps you in the home.

3. Sell on the traditional market

If you have equity and some time before the sale date, listing with a good local agent typically nets you the most money. The catch is time and condition: a market sale in the Orlando area usually means 30 to 60 days on market plus a 30-to-45-day closing, and buyers financing with a mortgage will expect the home to be in reasonable shape. If the auction is only weeks away or the house needs significant work, this route can be too slow — but when it fits, it's often the highest-dollar outcome.

4. Short sale

If you owe more than the home is worth, a short sale — where the lender agrees to accept less than the full balance — can be a graceful exit. It's slower because the lender has to approve the sale, and it does affect your credit, but it's generally less damaging than a completed foreclosure and can include a release from the deficiency. This is worth exploring when you're underwater and want to walk away cleanly.

5. Sell as-is to a cash buyer

When the auction is close, the house needs repairs, or you simply want certainty and speed, a direct cash sale can stop the foreclosure before it finishes. A cash buyer closes on your timeline — sometimes in as little as 7 to 14 days — with no repairs, no showings, and no financing that can fall through. The honest tradeoff: a cash offer is below full retail, generally in the range of 70–85% of the home's fully renovated market value, because the buyer takes on the repairs, costs, and risk. If you have equity, a fast cash sale can let you pay off the loan, protect that equity, and avoid a foreclosure on your record. If you don't, it may not beat a short sale. We'll always tell you honestly which one makes more sense for your numbers.

The one thing that helps most: acting while you still have choices. Every option above is easier and worth more the earlier you start. The week after you're served is a very different situation from the week before the auction.

How Long Do You Really Have?

Here's a rough Orlando timeline so you can locate yourself on the map:

  • Days 1–120 past due: Pre-foreclosure. The strongest window for modifications, forbearance, and unpressured sales.
  • Complaint filed: Foreclosure officially begins. Your 20-day clock to respond starts the day you're served.
  • Months 3–12: Motions, possible summary judgment, and the final judgment. A sale date gets set.
  • Auction: Redemption rights end when the certificate of sale is filed. Certificate of title follows about 10 days later.

The biggest variable is whether you respond and stay engaged. Homeowners who answer the complaint and communicate with their servicer routinely buy themselves months of additional time compared to those who don't.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my house in Orlando after the foreclosure lawsuit has already been filed?

Yes. As long as the certificate of sale has not been filed, you can still sell the home and pay off the loan through the closing. In fact, selling before the auction is one of the most common ways Orlando homeowners protect their remaining equity and avoid a completed foreclosure on their credit. The key is starting early enough that a closing can happen before the sale date.

Will I still owe money after a foreclosure in Florida?

You might, through a deficiency judgment, but Florida limits it. Under section 702.06, a deficiency on owner-occupied residential property generally can't exceed the difference between what you owed and the home's fair market value on the sale date, and the lender must request it within one year. A well-timed sale or short sale often reduces or eliminates that exposure, which is why understanding your numbers early is so valuable.

How much does foreclosure hurt my credit compared to a sale?

A completed foreclosure is one of the more serious marks on a credit report and can linger for years. A traditional sale, a short sale, or a cash sale that pays off the loan is generally less damaging and gives you a cleaner path to renting or buying again sooner. If protecting your credit matters, selling before the process finishes is almost always the better outcome.

What if I have almost no time before the auction?

You still have moves. Filing an answer to the complaint (if you haven't) can slow things down, reinstating pays off the arrears up until the certificate of sale, and a cash buyer can sometimes close fast enough to stop the sale. Even a single phone call to your servicer to request a short postponement of the sale date is sometimes granted when a sale is pending. Don't assume it's over until the certificate of title has actually been issued.
