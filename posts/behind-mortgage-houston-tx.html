Behind on Your Mortgage in Houston? Texas Foreclosure Process Explained

By Matt Vorwerk June 29, 2026 8 min read

Falling behind on your mortgage is one of the most stressful things a homeowner can go through. The mail starts piling up, the phone calls begin, and the words "notice of default" can make it feel like the walls are closing in. If that's where you are right now in Houston, the first thing worth saying is this: you have more time and more options than the letters make it seem. Texas foreclosure follows a defined process with clear deadlines, and understanding that process is the difference between reacting in a panic and making a calm, informed decision.

This guide walks through how foreclosure works in Texas, what the timeline looks like, what rights you have along the way, and five realistic paths out — whether you want to keep your home or move on with as much of your equity intact as possible. It's written from the perspective of someone who has sat across the kitchen table from a lot of Houston families in exactly this spot.

First, Understand How Texas Foreclosure Works

Texas is what's called a non-judicial foreclosure state. That's an important piece of vocabulary, so let's define it. In many states, a lender has to file a lawsuit and get a judge's permission before selling your home. In Texas, most mortgages are written as a "deed of trust" that includes a power of sale clause. That clause lets the lender foreclose without going to court, as long as it follows the steps spelled out in the Texas Property Code. Because no judge is supervising the process, the timeline moves faster than in judicial states — but the law still requires specific notices and waiting periods that protect you.

The whole non-judicial process is governed by Texas Property Code Section 51.002. The good news is that this statute is detailed and strict: if a lender skips a required notice or misses a deadline, the foreclosure can be challenged. Lenders know this, which is why the legitimate ones follow the timeline carefully.

The Texas Foreclosure Timeline, Step by Step

Here is how the process typically unfolds once you miss a payment. The exact pace varies by lender, but the legal milestones are fixed.

You miss a payment (and the early grace period)

A single missed payment doesn't start foreclosure. Most loans have a grace period of about 15 days, after which a late fee is charged. After roughly 30 days, your loan is reported delinquent. Foreclosure is still weeks away — but this is the cheapest and easiest time to act, so don't wait for the scary letters.

The federal 120-day rule

Before a servicer can start the formal foreclosure process, federal mortgage-servicing rules generally require you to be more than 120 days delinquent (this comes from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's servicing regulations, 12 CFR 1024.41). Those same federal rules give you the right to apply for "loss mitigation" — options like a loan modification or repayment plan — and the servicer generally can't move forward with a foreclosure sale while a complete application is under review. That 120-day window exists specifically to give you breathing room to work something out.

Notice of Default and Intent to Accelerate (the 20-day cure period)

Once you're seriously behind, the servicer sends a Notice of Default. Under Texas Property Code Section 51.002(d), for a loan on your primary residence the servicer must give you at least 20 days to "cure" — meaning to bring the loan current — before it can post the home for sale. This letter is sent by certified mail. Read it carefully: it states the amount you're behind and the date by which you must pay to stop the process.

Notice of Sale (at least 21 days before the auction)

If the default isn't cured within that window, the lender can accelerate the loan (demand the full balance) and issue a Notice of Sale. Under Section 51.002(b), this notice must be given at least 21 days before the sale date, and it must be done three ways: posted at the county courthouse, filed with the county clerk, and mailed to you by certified mail. In Harris County, that filing happens at the county clerk's office downtown.

Mark the date: Texas foreclosure auctions happen on the first Tuesday of the month, between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., at the county courthouse (or a designated area). So the 21-day notice points to a specific first Tuesday. Knowing your exact sale date tells you precisely how much time you have to act.

The foreclosure sale

On the first Tuesday, the property is sold at public auction to the highest bidder, often the lender itself. Unlike some states, Texas does not give homeowners a "right of redemption" to buy the home back after a standard mortgage foreclosure sale. (Two narrow exceptions exist — property-tax foreclosures and certain HOA-assessment foreclosures under Property Code Section 209.011 carry redemption rights — but a typical mortgage foreclosure does not.) That's why acting before the sale matters so much in Texas.

What About a Deficiency? (If the Home Sells for Less Than You Owe)

This is a question almost every homeowner asks, and it's an important one. If your home sells at foreclosure for less than the balance on your loan, the gap is called a deficiency. Texas law allows a lender to sue you for that deficiency, but there are real protections. Under Texas Property Code Section 51.003, the lender must file any deficiency lawsuit within two years of the foreclosure sale. And you have the right to ask the court to determine the property's fair market value as of the sale date — if that value is higher than the auction price, you're entitled to an offset that reduces what you owe. In practice, that means a lowball auction price doesn't automatically translate into a giant bill.

Selling the home yourself before the auction — whether on the market or to a cash buyer — is one of the most reliable ways to avoid a deficiency situation altogether, because you control the sale price instead of leaving it to a courthouse-steps auction.

Five Realistic Ways Out

Now the part that matters most: what can you actually do? Here are five paths Houston homeowners take. The right one depends on whether you want to keep the house and on how much time and equity you have.

1. Reinstate the loan

"Reinstating" means paying the total past-due amount — missed payments, late fees, and the lender's costs — in one lump sum to bring the loan current. In Texas you generally have the right to reinstate up until the foreclosure sale. If a tax refund, a family loan, or a bonus can cover the gap, this stops foreclosure cold and you keep your home and your original terms.

2. Apply for a loan modification or repayment plan

If the missed payments came from a temporary setback — a job loss, a medical event, a divorce — and your income has recovered, your servicer may agree to a loan modification (permanently changing your rate or term) or a repayment plan (spreading the past-due amount over several months). Call your servicer's loss-mitigation department early and in writing. Under the federal rules mentioned above, a complete, timely application generally pauses the sale while it's reviewed.

3. Forbearance

If your hardship is short-term, a forbearance agreement lets you pause or reduce payments for a set period, with a plan to catch up afterward. It's not forgiveness, but it buys time and stops the clock while you get back on your feet.

4. Sell the home on the open market

If you have equity and a little time before the sale date, listing with a Houston agent can net you the most money. The honest trade-off: a traditional sale takes time you may not have, and you'll typically pay 5–6% in commissions plus closing costs, on top of any repairs the home needs to show well. If your sale date is weeks away, this can be a tight squeeze.

5. Sell as-is to a direct cash buyer

When the auction is close, or the house needs work you can't afford, a direct cash sale can stop the foreclosure and protect whatever equity you have. There are no repairs, no showings, no agent commissions, and you can often close in as little as 7 to 14 days — fast enough to beat a first-Tuesday sale date. The trade-off, stated plainly: a cash offer is below full retail, because the buyer takes the home as-is and absorbs the repair and resale risk. When speed and certainty matter most, it's a fair option; when you have time and the home shows well, listing usually nets more. We'll tell you which one fits — even when the answer is "list it."

A note on scams: Foreclosure attracts bad actors. Be cautious of anyone who asks for an upfront fee to "save" your home, pressures you to sign over your deed, or tells you to stop talking to your lender. Legitimate buyers and HUD-approved housing counselors never charge desperate homeowners upfront fees.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does foreclosure take in Texas from the first missed payment?

It varies, but for most Houston homeowners the process from first missed payment to a foreclosure sale runs roughly four to six months. Federal rules generally keep the formal process from starting until you're more than 120 days delinquent, and once it does start, the 20-day cure notice and 21-day sale notice add at least several more weeks. That's faster than judicial-foreclosure states, but it's still enough time to act if you start early.

Can I stop the foreclosure if the sale date is next week?

Often, yes. In Texas you generally retain the right to reinstate the loan up until the sale, and a complete loss-mitigation application can pause the process. If keeping the home isn't realistic, a cash sale that closes in 7–14 days can sometimes beat the auction date entirely. The key is to call your servicer and explore your options immediately rather than waiting — every day counts as the first Tuesday approaches.

Will I still owe money after the house is foreclosed?

You might, if the home sells for less than your loan balance — that gap is the "deficiency." But Texas Property Code Section 51.003 requires the lender to sue within two years and lets you ask the court to credit the home's fair market value against what you owe, which often reduces or eliminates the deficiency. Selling the home yourself before the auction is the most reliable way to avoid a deficiency, because you control the price.

Does selling to a cash buyer hurt my credit less than foreclosure?

Generally, yes. A completed foreclosure is one of the most damaging marks on a credit report and can stay there for seven years. Selling the home — whether on the market or to a cash buyer — before a foreclosure is recorded means your loan is paid off rather than foreclosed, which is meaningfully easier on your credit and your ability to rent or buy again down the road.
