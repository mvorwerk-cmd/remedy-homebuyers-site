Selling an Inherited Home in Dallas: Texas Probate Made Simple

By Matt Vorwerk May 18, 2026 8 min read

Inheriting a house in Dallas is rarely as simple as someone handing you a set of keys. Between Texas probate rules, an unfamiliar property, out-of-town relatives, repairs nobody planned for, and a few tax surprises, most heirs feel underwater before they ever get to the front door. Take a breath — you have more options than you think, and you don't need to become a probate lawyer to make a good decision.

This guide walks through what actually happens when you inherit a property in Dallas — Lake Highlands, Oak Cliff, Lakewood, Pleasant Grove, Preston Hollow, or anywhere else in the county — and decide to sell. Plain English, no jargon.

Step 1: Figure Out Which Texas Probate Path Applies

Texas is one of the easier states in the country for probate, but you still have to pick the right door. Which path applies depends on how the deceased held title and whether they left a valid will.

  • Living trust. If the home was titled in a properly funded revocable living trust, it usually avoids probate completely. The successor trustee can sell the property directly once they record an affidavit of successor trustee with Dallas County.
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship or community property with right of survivorship. Title passes automatically to the surviving owner. A certified death certificate filed in the Dallas County deed records is typically enough to clear title for sale.
  • Transfer-on-death deed. Texas allows TOD deeds for residential property under the Texas Real Property Transfer on Death Act. If the previous owner recorded one, the named beneficiary takes title without probate.
  • Will, no debts beyond a mortgage — "Muniment of Title." A uniquely Texas option (Texas Estates Code Chapter 257). If the will is valid and the estate has no unpaid debts other than a mortgage, the court can admit the will as a muniment in a single hearing — no executor, no inventory. Typical cost and time: a few hundred dollars in court fees and 30 to 60 days.
  • Will with debts, or any complexity — "Independent Administration." Texas's default form of probate (Texas Estates Code Chapter 401). The court appoints an independent executor who can sell the home without further court orders once the inventory is filed. Timeline: 6 to 12 months.
  • Small Estate Affidavit. Texas Estates Code Chapter 205 allows heirs to skip probate when the total estate (excluding homestead and exempt property) is $75,000 or less and the deceased died without a will. Talk to a Dallas probate attorney about whether your situation fits.
  • Dependent Administration. The slow, court-supervised version, used when heirs are fighting or when the will requires it. Every sale needs court approval. Plan on 12 to 24 months.

Reality check: Dallas County's probate courts (Probate Courts 1, 2, and 3) are some of the busiest in the state. Even a smooth muniment of title can take 30 to 60 days to schedule a hearing. You generally cannot transfer clean title to a buyer until the court order is signed — but an experienced cash buyer can hold the contract open while you wait.

Step 2: Understand the Stepped-Up Tax Basis (It's Actually Good News)

Here's the rare piece of tax law that works in your favor: under Internal Revenue Code §1014, when you inherit real estate, your cost basis "steps up" to the fair market value on the date of the original owner's death. If your father bought the house in East Dallas in 1981 for $48,000 and it was worth $385,000 the day he passed, your basis is $385,000 — not $48,000.

Why this matters: if you sell quickly for around its date-of-death value, you'll likely owe little or no federal capital gains tax. Texas helps even more — there is no state income tax, so you don't owe Texas a dime of capital gains either. Get a written appraisal or broker's opinion of value dated as close to the date of death as possible, and keep it in the estate file.

Step 3: Decide What to Actually Do With the House

Heirs generally land in one of four camps. None of these is wrong — the right answer depends on your life, not a rulebook.

Option A: Move into it

If the house suits your life, this is the simplest path. Texas's homestead exemption (Texas Tax Code Chapter 11) can cut your property taxes meaningfully once you establish residence, and there is no reassessment on inheritance the way California and a handful of other states impose. Just file the homestead exemption application with the Dallas Central Appraisal District in the first year.

Option B: Rent it out

Dallas's rental market is strong, especially for single-family homes in Lake Highlands, Casa Linda, M Streets, and parts of Pleasant Grove. But being a landlord is a real job, and doing it from a distance is rough. Rough math: if the house needs more than about $15,000 of work to be rent-ready, renting usually isn't the fastest path to cash.

Option C: Fix it up and sell on the traditional market

This path produces the highest price on paper. But "on paper" is doing real work in that sentence. You'll typically need $10,000 to $60,000 for repairs, 20 to 60 days on market, 5–6% in agent commissions, 1–2% in closing costs, plus monthly holding costs (Texas property taxes are not gentle). For an heir living out of state or juggling a full-time job, the logistics are exhausting.

Option D: Sell as-is to a cash buyer

A direct cash sale skips the repairs, the listings, the showings, and the financing contingencies. You usually have a written offer within 24 to 48 hours, pick your closing date (often 7 to 21 days, or whenever probate allows), and walk away with a check. The tradeoff is the offer is below retail — generally 70–85% of what the home would sell for fully renovated on the MLS — because the buyer is absorbing the risk, repairs, and holding costs.

Step 4: Texas Disclosure Rules for Inherited Sales

Texas Property Code §5.008 requires sellers to give buyers a Seller's Disclosure Notice — but subsection (e) carves out an exception for transfers "by a fiduciary in the course of the administration of a decedent's estate." If you're selling as the executor or administrator and you never lived in the house, you're typically exempt from filling out the standard disclosure. You still have to disclose any material defects you actually know about (leaking roof, foundation issue, prior flooding); you just aren't required to investigate the home's history.

Step 5: Handle the Stuff Inside the House

Most heirs underestimate this. A lifetime of belongings can take days or weeks to sort through, and Texas requires you to preserve anything that could be evidence in a will contest until the estate is closed. Practical tips:

  • Take a walk-through video on your phone before touching anything.
  • Check every drawer, closet, coat pocket, and book for cash, jewelry, deeds, and insurance policies before donating or discarding.
  • Estate sale companies in DFW typically take 25–40% of gross proceeds.
  • Whole-house cleanouts run $1,500–$4,500. A good cash buyer will often take the house with the contents inside — saving you the cleanout entirely.

Step 6: Navigate the Family Dynamics

If you're not the only heir, this step is usually harder than all the others combined. Everyone has a different financial situation, a different emotional attachment to the house, and a different idea of what it's worth. A few ground rules that tend to keep families from blowing apart:

  • Get one independent valuation everyone agrees on upfront. Don't argue about the price — argue about which appraiser to use, then accept whatever number they produce.
  • Decide early whether you're selling or keeping. Mixed goals ("I want to keep it, you want to sell") almost always end in a forced sale anyway, usually at a worse price after months of fighting.
  • Put the agreement in writing, even informally. Email is fine. Verbal promises between siblings disappear under stress.
  • If one heir wants to buy out the others, use the same independent valuation and include a small premium (5–10%) for the convenience of avoiding a market sale and the months of holding costs.

How Long Does the Whole Process Take in Dallas?

Best case (trust or muniment of title, no repairs, cash sale): 30 to 45 days from court hearing to closing.

Typical case (independent administration, light cleanup, cash sale): 60 to 120 days.

Full dependent administration plus traditional MLS sale: 12 to 24 months.

The single biggest variable is which probate path applies. If the estate is sitting in Dallas County Probate Court, you're waiting on the court — not on the market — for the front portion of the timeline regardless of how you eventually sell.

Common Mistakes We See Dallas Heirs Make

  • Waiting too long to list. Every month the house sits empty in DFW costs roughly $1,500–$3,000 in taxes, insurance, utilities, and lawn care — plus the rising risk of vandalism on a vacant property.
  • Pouring money into repairs that don't return. Do the cheap stuff (paint, landscaping, deep cleaning); skip the big renovations unless an agent shows you comps that justify them.
  • Accepting the first offer without understanding it. Ask for proof of funds, confirm the buyer is a real company (not a wholesaler reselling your contract), and get the offer in writing.
  • Skipping the date-of-death valuation. Without it, your CPA has to estimate basis later and the IRS gets to argue the number.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell an inherited house in Dallas before probate is finished?

Sometimes, yes. If the estate is in independent administration, the executor can usually sign a sale contract and close after the inventory is approved — often within 60 to 90 days of filing. If the will qualifies for muniment of title, you can typically close as soon as the muniment order is signed (often 30 to 60 days). What you generally cannot do is transfer clean title before any court order at all. A cash buyer who has done Dallas probate deals before will sign the contract now and wait for the order, which gives you certainty without rushing the court.

Do I owe capital gains tax when I sell an inherited home in Texas?

Usually very little, and never to Texas. Your federal basis is the fair market value on the date of death under IRC §1014, so if you sell near that price you owe federal tax only on any gain above it. Texas has no state income tax, so there's no state capital gains either. Talk to a CPA before you sign.

What is "muniment of title" and is it really faster?

Muniment of title (Texas Estates Code Chapter 257) is a Texas-only shortcut. With a valid will and no unpaid debts other than a mortgage, the court admits the will as a deed-like document that transfers title to the named beneficiaries — no executor, no inventory. Most Dallas-area muniments close in 30 to 60 days. It really is faster, for estates that qualify.

What if multiple heirs disagree about selling?

If you can't agree, any heir can file a partition action under Texas Property Code Chapter 23 to force a sale. Partition is slow (9 to 18 months), expensive, and the court-ordered sale typically nets less than a voluntary one. Most families would have done better agreeing to a single buyout valuation at the start. A mediator or a neutral cash buyer can sometimes break the impasse by giving everyone the same number to react to.
