Selling a Tenant-Occupied House in Houston: Texas Landlord and Buyer Rules

By Matt Vorwerk November 9, 2026 8 min read

Yes, you can sell a tenant-occupied house in Houston, and you do not have to evict the tenant first. In Texas, you can sell with a tenant in place, and the buyer generally takes the house subject to the existing lease, while you can also deliver it vacant if you follow the Texas eviction process. Which route fits depends on the lease, the rent history, and how fast you need to close.

Owning a rental that has become a burden is common: a tenant who stopped paying, a property that needs repairs, or an inherited house you never planned to manage. This guide walks through the Texas rules for landlords and buyers, what it costs to evict versus sell as-is, and when a cash sale is, and is not, your best option.

Key Takeaways

  • You can sell a Houston rental with the tenant still living in it; you are not required to evict first.
  • Under Texas Property Code Chapter 24, a landlord generally must give at least 3 days' written notice to vacate before filing an eviction suit, unless the lease sets a different period.
  • A new owner becomes responsible for tenant security deposits from the date title transfers, under Tex. Prop. Code § 92.105.
  • Eviction in Texas is decided in the justice court, and either side generally has 5 days after judgment to appeal.
  • Never lock out or shut off utilities to force a tenant out; Texas limits lockouts under § 92.0081.

Can You Sell a Tenant-Occupied House in Houston, Texas?

A Houston landlord can legally sell a rental house while the tenant lives there. The sale does not, by itself, cancel the lease. In most cases the buyer steps into the landlord's shoes, takes over the lease terms and the rent, and becomes responsible for the tenant's rights from closing forward.

That is why the lease matters so much. A signed lease with 10 months left is a very different asset than a month-to-month tenancy. Gather the lease, any addenda, the rent ledger, the security deposit amount and any notices you have sent before you talk to a buyer or agent.

What Happens to the Lease and the Security Deposit When You Sell?

Under Texas law, the new owner is responsible for returning a tenant's security deposit once the owner's interest changes hands. Texas Property Code § 92.105 addresses what happens to security deposits when a landlord's interest ends by sale, and a former owner can remain exposed to the tenant until the buyer has received the deposit or assumed the obligation. In practice, deposits are credited to the buyer on the closing statement.

Do not skip this step. If you sell and the deposit never reaches the buyer, the tenant may still look to you. Ask the title company to list the deposit as a credit and give the tenant written notice of the new owner and where to send rent. Landlords are also expected to keep ownership and management information current for tenants under Chapter 92 of the Property Code.

How Does Eviction Work in Houston?

Eviction in Texas is a court process, and a landlord cannot simply remove a tenant. It starts with a written notice to vacate, then a suit in the justice court for the precinct where the property sits, which in Houston is a Harris County justice court. Only a constable can carry out a court-ordered removal.

The notice to vacate

Under Texas Property Code § 24.005, a landlord generally must give a tenant at least 3 days' written notice to vacate before filing an eviction suit, unless the lease sets a shorter or longer period. The Legislature revisited this section in 2025 with Senate Bill 38, which also changed how evictions are handled. Because the details of delivery and timing changed, have a Texas attorney or the court clerk confirm the current rules before you serve anything.

The suit, judgment and appeal

After the notice period, the landlord files a petition in justice court, and the case is heard on a fast schedule set by Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 510. If the tenant loses, they can appeal to the county court by following Rule 510.19, generally within 5 days of the judgment. An appeal can keep a case alive for weeks longer, so plan for delay even in a case you expect to win.

In our experience, a straightforward nonpayment eviction can take several weeks from notice to possession, and a contested case takes longer. Add legal fees, court costs, lost rent while the case runs, and repairs after move-out, and a house you hoped to sell quickly may be off the market for months.

What Rules Must Houston Landlords Follow While Selling?

Selling does not suspend a landlord's duties. Until closing, the tenant still has the same repair rights and protections as before. In particular, Texas restricts when a landlord can change locks on a tenant who is behind on rent; Tex. Prop. Code § 92.0081 requires specific notice if a lock is changed for delinquent rent, and a landlord cannot use a lockout to avoid the court process.

Showings are another friction point. Most Texas leases require you to give reasonable notice before entering, and a tenant who feels pressured may stop cooperating. Read the lease's entry clause and put every showing request in writing. Cash buyers like us typically need only one or two walk-throughs, which is one reason a cash sale is easier on an occupied house.

What If the Tenant Is Behind on Rent or the House Is Damaged?

Back rent does not automatically transfer to the buyer. Unless the contract says otherwise, unpaid rent you are owed stays your claim, so decide before closing whether you will assign it to the buyer or pursue it yourself. A damaged house is a separate issue: a buyer who purchases as-is prices the repairs into the offer instead of asking you to fix them.

If you are also behind on the mortgage, tenants may be entitled to protection if the lender forecloses. The federal Protecting Tenants at Foreclosure Act generally gives a bona fide tenant at least 90 days' notice before a successor owner can require them to leave. A cash buyer will usually offer less than a repaired, vacant house would bring on the open market, because we take on the tenant, the repairs, and the risk. We say so up front. If your tenant is reliable, the house is in good condition, and you can wait, listing the house vacant or selling to an investor may net you more. A cash sale makes the most sense when the tenant situation, the condition, or the clock is the real problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to evict my tenant before selling my house in Houston?

No. Texas law does not require you to evict a tenant before selling. You can sell with the tenant in place, and the buyer generally takes the property subject to the lease. You would only evict first if you want to deliver the house vacant, which adds court time, cost and risk.

How much notice must a Texas landlord give before evicting a tenant?

Under Texas Property Code § 24.005, a landlord generally must give at least 3 days' written notice to vacate before filing an eviction suit, unless the lease provides a different period. Rules on delivery and timing were revisited in 2025, so confirm the current requirements with a Texas attorney or the justice court clerk.

What happens to my tenant's security deposit if I sell?

The new owner becomes responsible for returning the deposit once the landlord's interest transfers, under Texas Property Code § 92.105. As the seller, you can remain exposed until the buyer receives the deposit or assumes the obligation, so have the title company credit it to the buyer at closing.

Can a cash buyer purchase a house that has a tenant in it?

Yes. Many cash buyers purchase occupied houses as-is, with the lease in place or with a negotiated move-out. The price reflects the tenant situation and condition, so expect an offer below a vacant, repaired house, but you avoid eviction costs, showings and repairs.

Can I lock out or shut off utilities for a tenant who won't leave?

No. A Texas landlord cannot use self-help to remove a tenant, and lockouts for delinquent rent are tightly limited by Property Code § 92.0081. The lawful route is a written notice to vacate followed by an eviction suit in justice court. Violations can expose you to liability, so speak with an attorney first.
