Inherited a House in Philadelphia? A Plain-English Guide to PA Probate

By Matt Vorwerk September 28, 2026 9 min read

A Philadelphia rowhouse is often the most valuable thing a family owns, and when the person whose name was on the deed passes away, it can also become the most complicated. Maybe you grew up in the Germantown twin your grandmother bought in 1971. Maybe you're the only one of four siblings still close enough to check on the Mayfair house. Maybe you inherited a property in Overbrook and have never seen the inside.

Here's what actually happens when you inherit a house in Philadelphia: how Pennsylvania probate works, the two taxes that surprise people, the city's unusually common "tangled title" problem, and four honest options for the property. We're cash buyers, not attorneys — and we'll say plainly when selling to a company like ours is not your best move.

First Question: Does the House Have to Go Through Probate?

Probate is the court-supervised process of proving a will and transferring a deceased person's property to the people entitled to it. In Pennsylvania it runs through the Register of Wills in the county where the person lived — in Philadelphia, the office at City Hall, with disputes heard by the Orphans' Court Division. Whether the house needs probate depends entirely on how the deed was written:

  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (or tenancy by the entirety between spouses): Title passes automatically to the surviving owner — no probate for the house.
  • Revocable living trust: If the deed was actually transferred into a properly funded trust, the successor trustee can sell directly.
  • Sole ownership, with or without a will: Probate is required before the property can be sold or refinanced. This is the most common situation we see in Philadelphia.
  • A "transfer-on-death deed": Pennsylvania does not authorize these for real estate. Legislation to adopt the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act has been introduced but not enacted. If someone recorded a TOD deed on a Philadelphia property, assume it does nothing and the house still needs probate.

A common misunderstanding: Pennsylvania's small-estate shortcut under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3102 lets the Orphans' Court distribute an estate's personal property worth $50,000 or less. Real estate is expressly excluded from that calculation, and the petition does not transfer the house. If the deed is in the deceased person's name alone, you need letters from the Register of Wills no matter how modest the estate.

Philadelphia's "Tangled Title" Problem Is Real — and Fixable

A tangled title means you live in a house, pay the taxes, maintain the roof — and your name isn't on the deed, usually because a parent or grandparent died and probate was never completed. A 2020 Philadelphia VIP analysis identified at least 14,000 such properties; a later Pew study put the number above 10,000, clustered hardest in long-tenured neighborhoods like Kensington, Point Breeze, Germantown, and Frankford.

It's more than paperwork. Without clear title you generally cannot sell, refinance, get a home-repair loan, or claim the city's Homestead Exemption — and families sometimes lose houses at sheriff's sale over a few thousand dollars in back taxes they couldn't enter a payment agreement for.

The good news: Philadelphia has built real infrastructure here. The Register of Wills runs a Title Clearance Unit that walks qualifying residents through probate, and the city funds a Tangled Title Program connecting low-income homeowners with free legal help. Philadelphia VIP and Philadelphia Legal Assistance both take these cases. If yours is a tangled title rather than a straightforward estate, start there before talking to anybody about selling.

How Philadelphia Probate Actually Works, Step by Step

  1. Open the estate. The executor named in the will (or, with no will, the closest next of kin) petitions the Register of Wills and receives letters testamentary or letters of administration. That document is your legal authority to act — title companies will ask for it.
  2. Advertise the grant of letters. Pennsylvania requires notice published once a week for three weeks: in a county newspaper of general circulation and in the county's legal periodical, which in Philadelphia is The Legal Intelligencer.
  3. Inventory the estate. File an inventory of assets valued as of the date of death. That date-of-death figure for the house matters enormously for taxes.
  4. Deal with debts. Mortgages, liens, water bills, and unpaid property taxes follow the house. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3532, a personal representative who distributes property is protected from claims not made known within one year after the first complete advertisement of the grant of letters — which is why attorneys often advise waiting out that year before final distribution, even though the house can usually be sold sooner.
  5. File the inheritance tax return. Pennsylvania's REV-1500 is due nine months from the date of death.
  6. Sell or transfer, then close the estate. The executor signs the deed on the estate's behalf; if heirs already took title by deed of distribution, they sign individually.

A clean Philadelphia estate with a valid will and cooperative heirs takes roughly 9 to 14 months to close — but you rarely have to wait that long to sell. Once letters are issued, a sale can typically close in weeks. Contested estates, missing heirs, or titles tangled two generations back can take years.

Two Taxes That Surprise Philadelphia Heirs

1. Pennsylvania inheritance tax

Pennsylvania is one of a handful of states that still taxes inheritances, and unlike the federal estate tax there's no large exemption to hide behind. The rate depends on your relationship to the person who died: 0% for a surviving spouse, 4.5% for lineal descendants such as children and grandchildren, 12% for siblings, 15% for everyone else — nieces, nephews, cousins, friends.

Two practical notes. The tax is assessed on the date-of-death value, not on what you sell for. And Pennsylvania gives a 5% discount on tax paid within three months of death: on a $250,000 rowhouse passing to a child, the 4.5% tax is $11,250, and paying early saves roughly $560. Many families prepay partially just to capture it.

2. Philadelphia realty transfer tax

Philadelphia's realty transfer tax rose to 3.578% on July 1, 2025. Added to the 1% Commonwealth rate, the combined tax is 4.578% of the sale price — among the highest in the nation. By local custom it's split between buyer and seller, but who pays is negotiable and belongs in the agreement of sale. The relief: transfers to heirs by will or intestacy are excluded, so moving the deed from your mother's estate into your name isn't taxable. The tax applies when the property sells to a third party.

And genuinely good news: the stepped-up basis

When you inherit real estate, your cost basis for capital-gains purposes "steps up" to fair market value on the date of death. If your father bought the Roxborough house in 1979 for $28,000 and it was worth $265,000 when he died, your basis is $265,000 — sell at that price and there's essentially no capital gain to report. Sell a year later for $285,000 and you owe tax only on the $20,000 of post-death gain. That's why a defensible date-of-death valuation is worth paying for: get a formal appraisal dated as close to the death as possible and keep it in the estate file.

Do You Have to Fill Out a Seller's Disclosure?

Pennsylvania's Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law normally requires detailed written disclosure of known material defects. Transfers by a fiduciary administering a decedent's estate are excluded under 68 Pa.C.S. § 7302, so an executor selling on the estate's behalf generally doesn't have to complete the standard form.

Don't read that as license for silence. If you know about a material defect — the basement takes water every spring, the roof leaks, the heater was red-tagged — disclose it in writing. And once heirs take title individually and sell as owners rather than fiduciaries, the exemption may not apply at all.

Four Realistic Ways to Handle the Property

Option A: List it with an agent

This produces the highest top-line price, and for a structurally sound house in a strong submarket — Roxborough, Fishtown, parts of Mayfair — it's often the right call. Budget 5–6% in commissions, 1–2% in closing costs, your share of the transfer tax, and whatever the house needs to show well. FHA and conventional buyers bring an appraiser and an inspector, so deferred maintenance becomes a negotiation. Expect 30–90 days on market plus 30–45 to close.

Option B: Sell it yourself (FSBO)

You save the listing-side commission but will likely still pay a buyer's agent, and you're handling showings, disclosures, and title issues personally. Works best when you already have a buyer — a neighbor, a tenant, a family friend. Works poorly from out of state.

Option C: Keep it and rent it out

Philadelphia rowhouse rents are respectable and holding lets the property keep appreciating. But the city requires a rental license and Certificate of Rental Suitability, lead-safe certification applies to most pre-1978 rentals with young children, and long-distance landlording on a 90-year-old house is real work. Rough rule: if it needs more than about $15,000 to be rent-ready, renting isn't the calmest path.

Option D: Sell as-is to a cash buyer

A direct cash sale skips repairs, showings, appraisals, and financing contingencies. You choose the closing date, we pay closing costs, and the house can be sold with the contents still inside. The honest tradeoff: the offer is below retail — typically 70–85% of what the home would fetch fully renovated — because the buyer absorbs the repairs, carrying costs, and risk. If the house is in decent shape and you have time and patience, listing nets you more. If it needs $40,000 of work or four heirs need this resolved before it damages the family, the certainty is often worth the discount.

Worth knowing either way: an empty Philadelphia rowhouse typically costs $900 to $1,900 a month to hold — taxes, vacant-property insurance, water and sewer, heat kept on so the pipes don't burst, exterior upkeep. That's not a reason to rush a bad decision, but it is a reason to make a decision rather than letting the estate drift for two years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell an inherited Philadelphia house before probate is finished?

In most cases, yes. What you need is legal authority — letters testamentary or letters of administration — not a fully closed estate. Once letters are issued, the executor can sign an agreement of sale and convey title, with a title company confirming the paper trail. What you cannot do is sell before the estate is opened, which is exactly the trap a tangled title creates.

What happens if my siblings and I disagree about selling?

If the house passes to several heirs and one refuses to sell, the others can file a partition action in the Court of Common Pleas, which ultimately forces a sale and splits the proceeds. It's slow, expensive, and lawyers take a share. Far better: agree on one independent appraisal before anybody argues about price, decide early whether the goal is selling or keeping, and put the agreement in writing — an email chain beats memory.

Do I have to pay off the mortgage or back taxes before I can sell?

No — those come out of the proceeds at closing, same as any other sale. Mortgages, city tax liens, water liens, and L&I violations attach to the property and get settled through the title company's payoff process. The real problem is when the house is worth less than what's owed, which does happen in parts of the city. Then a short sale or deed in lieu may be more realistic — talk to both the lender and an attorney.
