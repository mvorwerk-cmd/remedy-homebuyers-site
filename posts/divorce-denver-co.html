Divorce and Your Denver Home: How Colorado Couples Split Real Estate in 2026

By Matt Vorwerk June 22, 2026 8 min read

For most divorcing couples in Denver, the house is the single biggest thing they own together — and the hardest to untangle. It holds equity, memories, and often a mortgage that was approved based on two incomes. Deciding what happens to it can feel paralyzing, especially while you're also sorting out the rest of your life.

The good news is that Colorado law is more predictable than most people expect, and you usually have more paths forward than the two everyone assumes ("I keep it" or "you keep it"). This guide explains, in plain English, how Colorado courts treat real estate in a divorce, what the timeline really looks like, and the four realistic ways couples handle the family home. It's written from the perspective of someone who has helped many local families work through this exact decision.

First, How Colorado Actually Divides Property

Colorado is an equitable distribution state, not a community property state. That distinction matters. Community property states (like neighboring states to the west) split marital assets straight down the middle. Colorado instead divides marital property in proportions the court considers fair — which is often close to 50/50, but doesn't have to be.

The rules come from Colorado's Uniform Dissolution of Marriage Act, specifically C.R.S. § 14-10-113. Under that statute, the court sets aside each spouse's separate property and then divides the marital property "without regard to marital misconduct." In other words, who caused the divorce generally has no bearing on who gets the house. The court weighs factors such as each spouse's contribution to acquiring the property (including the contribution of a spouse who worked as a homemaker), the economic circumstances of each spouse, and the desirability of awarding the family home to the spouse who has primary care of the children.

Plain-English version: Colorado aims for a fair split of what you built together during the marriage. "Fair" sometimes means equal and sometimes doesn't — a judge has real discretion, which is exactly why most couples settle this themselves rather than leave it to the court.

Is the House Even "Marital Property"?

Before you can divide the home, you have to classify it. Under C.R.S. § 14-10-113, marital property is generally everything either spouse acquired during the marriage. Separate property includes anything owned before the marriage, plus property received by gift or inheritance during the marriage. Separate property stays with the spouse it belongs to.

Here's where Denver homes get complicated:

  • Bought during the marriage: Almost always fully marital property, even if only one spouse is on the deed or the loan.
  • Owned by one spouse before the marriage: The home itself may stay separate, but in Colorado the increase in value during the marriage is treated as marital property and gets divided. In a market like Denver's, that appreciation can be substantial.
  • Inherited or gifted to one spouse: Generally separate — but only if it stayed separate. If you added your spouse to the title, deposited rental income into a joint account, or used marital funds for the mortgage or a remodel, you may have "commingled" it into marital property.

The lesson: don't assume. A home you think is "yours" may be partly your spouse's once Colorado's appreciation and commingling rules are applied. This is the one area where a short consult with a family-law attorney almost always pays for itself.

The Timeline: Colorado's 91-Day Waiting Period

Even the friendliest, most agreed-upon divorce can't be rushed in Colorado. Under C.R.S. § 14-10-106, a court cannot finalize a dissolution of marriage until at least 91 days have passed from the date the other spouse was served (or from the date both spouses filed jointly). This waiting period is mandatory and cannot be waived, no matter how amicable you are.

In practice, an uncontested Denver divorce often finalizes in roughly three to six months; a contested one can take a year or more. That timeline matters for the house, because it affects how long you'll both keep paying the mortgage, taxes, and insurance before anything is resolved. The longer the case drags, the more those monthly costs eat into the equity you're trying to divide.

The Four Ways Denver Couples Handle the Marital Home

Once you know roughly what the house is worth and how much of it is marital, the decision usually comes down to one of four options. None is automatically right — the best choice depends on your finances, your kids, and how quickly you both want a clean break.

Option A: One spouse buys the other out

If one of you wants to stay, that spouse pays the other their share of the equity and refinances the mortgage into their own name alone. The catch in 2026 is interest rates: if your current loan carries a low pandemic-era rate, refinancing means trading it for today's higher rate, which can sharply raise the monthly payment. The staying spouse also has to qualify for the new loan on a single income. Run those numbers before you fall in love with this option.

Option B: Sell on the open market and split the proceeds

Listing the home with an agent typically produces the highest sale price, and a clean sale gives both spouses cash and a fresh start. The trade-offs are time and cooperation: you'll need to agree on repairs, a list price, and showing schedules while living separate lives, plus absorb agent commissions (usually 5–6%), closing costs, and months of shared carrying costs. For couples who can still cooperate and aren't in a hurry, this often nets the most money.

Option C: Keep co-owning it for now

Some couples agree to hold the home temporarily — often so children can finish a school year in neighborhoods like Park Hill or Washington Park, or to wait out a slow market. This can work, but put every detail in writing: who pays the mortgage, who handles repairs, when you'll sell, and how you'll split proceeds. An informal "we'll figure it out later" between former spouses tends to unravel under stress.

Option D: Sell as-is to a cash buyer

When speed and certainty matter more than squeezing out the last dollar — for example, when neither spouse can afford the payment alone, the home needs repairs neither wants to fund, or you both simply want it over with — a direct cash sale can be the cleanest exit. You skip repairs, showings, and financing contingencies, pick a closing date that fits the court's timeline, and divide a known, agreed-upon number. The honest trade-off is that a cash offer comes in below full retail (typically 70–85% of the renovated market value), because the buyer absorbs the repairs, holding costs, and risk. For some couples that discount is well worth the speed and the absence of conflict; for others it isn't. We'll always tell you which camp we think you're in.

A note on fairness: Whatever path you choose, get one independent valuation you both accept before negotiating. Fighting over the number is where divorces get expensive. Agree on who appraises the home, then accept the figure they produce and divide from there.

What Selling Looks Like in the Denver Market

Denver real estate has cooled from its frenzied peak but remains relatively strong, which generally works in a divorcing couple's favor when there's equity to divide. Demand and price vary a lot by neighborhood. Established areas like Washington Park, Cherry Creek, and the Highlands (LoHi) tend to hold value and draw move-in-ready buyers, while homes in Park Hill and the Central Park (formerly Stapleton) area see steady family demand. If your home needs significant work, though, the gap between a polished listing and an as-is sale narrows — buyers in any neighborhood discount heavily for deferred maintenance.

Don't Forget the Disclosures

If you do sell, Colorado expects sellers to be honest about the property's condition. The standard Seller's Property Disclosure form used across the state asks you to disclose known material defects — roof issues, foundation problems, past flooding, and the like. During a divorce, neither spouse may know the home as well as they think, especially if one moved out years earlier. Compare notes and disclose what you both know; failing to disclose a known defect can create liability that follows you long after the marriage is over. (A genuine benefit of an as-is cash sale is that the buyer accepts the property's condition directly, which simplifies this step considerably.)

Common Mistakes We See During Divorce Sales

  • Letting the mortgage lapse out of spite or confusion. Until the divorce is final and the loan is refinanced or paid off, both names on the loan are still responsible. A missed payment damages both credit scores.
  • Assuming whoever's on the deed keeps the equity. Colorado divides marital equity regardless of whose name is on title. The deed doesn't settle the question — the divorce decree does.
  • Overspending on repairs to "win" a higher price. In a contested split, big renovations rarely return their cost and just give you one more thing to fight about. Do the cheap, high-impact items (paint, cleaning, landscaping) or sell as-is.
  • Dragging out the sale. Every extra month of shared mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities shrinks the equity you're dividing — and prolongs the stress for everyone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we have to sell the house if we get divorced in Denver?

No. Selling is only one of several options. One spouse can buy the other out and refinance, you can agree to co-own the home temporarily, or you can sell — either on the open market or as-is to a cash buyer. A court will usually order a sale only if you can't agree and no one can afford to keep the home alone.

Is Colorado a 50/50 state for property in a divorce?

Not exactly. Colorado is an equitable-distribution state under C.R.S. § 14-10-113, meaning marital property is divided fairly rather than automatically in half. In many cases the split ends up close to equal, but a judge can weigh factors like each spouse's finances and who has primary care of the children to reach a different result.

How fast can we sell the house if we both just want it over?

An on-market sale typically takes 30–90 days plus closing, on top of the time to prep and list. An as-is cash sale can close in as little as 7–14 days, though you'll still need the court's sign-off to finalize the divorce itself thanks to Colorado's mandatory 91-day waiting period. Many couples sell the home first and let the proceeds sit in escrow until the decree is entered.

What happens to the equity if only one of us is on the mortgage?

If the home was bought during the marriage, the equity is generally marital property and gets divided no matter whose name is on the loan or deed. Being the only borrower means you're solely responsible for the payments until the loan is refinanced or paid off — but it doesn't mean you automatically keep all the value.
