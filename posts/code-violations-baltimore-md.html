Selling a Baltimore House with Code Violations: Maryland Options

By Matt Vorwerk November 16, 2026 8 min read

Yes, you can sell a Baltimore house with code violations, and you do not have to fix them first. Under Baltimore City Building Code § 114.23, a buyer must get a violation report before closing and then becomes primarily liable for the open notices. What you must do is disclose: a Vacant Building Notice has to be disclosed in the contract of sale, and Maryland law requires disclosure of known latent defects even in an as-is sale.

Violation notices tend to arrive at the worst time: after a death in the family, during a long vacancy, or when a rental has fallen behind on repairs. This guide explains in plain English how Baltimore code enforcement works, what a Vacant Building Notice means for a sale, what you owe the buyer, and how to compare your options honestly.

Key Takeaways

  • Baltimore lets you sell with open violations; the buyer takes on the notices under Building Code § 114.23.
  • Since 2021 (Council Bill 21-0078), sellers must disclose a Vacant Building Notice in the contract of sale.
  • An owner can be fined $1,000 for not removing a Vacant Building Notice, per Baltimore DHCD.
  • Maryland Real Property § 10-702 requires you to disclose known latent defects, even when you sell as-is.
  • Baltimore City charges a 1.5% transfer tax on the sale price, per the Bureau of Revenue Collections.

What Counts as a Code Violation in Baltimore?

A code violation in Baltimore is a written finding by the city that a property does not meet the Baltimore City Building, Fire, and Related Codes, which include the city's Property Maintenance Code. The Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD) handles property maintenance and code enforcement, and an inspector who finds a problem issues a violation notice to the owner.

Common examples include a roof that leaks, broken windows or doors, missing smoke detectors, exterior trash and overgrowth, unsafe stairs, and electrical or plumbing hazards. A violation notice tells you what is wrong and gives a deadline to correct it. A citation is a separate step that carries a fine, and the owner must either pay it or request a hearing.

Baltimore's challenge is scale. The city has lost more than 350,000 residents since 1950, and as of February 15, 2022 it counted 14,989 vacant properties, nearly all privately owned, according to the DHCD vacant building FAQ. That history is why enforcement around vacant houses is so active.

What Is a Vacant Building Notice, and Why Does It Matter When You Sell?

A Vacant Building Notice (VBN) is a violation notice Baltimore places on a property that is vacant, abandoned, and uninhabitable. It is not the same as a house simply sitting empty. A VBN means the city has found the building unfit to live in, and it stays on the property until the owner rehabs the house and obtains a final Use and Occupancy permit.

According to DHCD, the owner can be fined $1,000 for not removing a VBN. Since Council Bill 21-0078 passed in 2021, a seller must disclose a VBN as part of the contract of sale, and the city urges buyers, agents and title companies to check for one before signing. A VBN limits who can buy: most mortgage lenders will not finance an uninhabitable house, so buyers are usually investors paying cash or using renovation financing.

How to check your property

Gather every notice and citation you have received. Then look up the address on the city's CoDeMap tool, which DHCD points buyers to for VBN status. Your title company will order a city lien certificate from the Bureau of Revenue Collections, which shows unpaid city charges such as water bills, fines and cleaning or boarding costs that must be settled at closing.

Can You Sell a Baltimore House Without Fixing the Violations?

Baltimore does not require a seller to repair code violations before a sale. Instead, the Building Code places the obligation on the buyer. Under § 114.23, before any transfer by sale, assignment, ground rent lease or otherwise, the buyer must get a violation report for the property. Notices listed on that report become the buyer's responsibility, the same as if they had been addressed to the buyer, and the buyer must abate them.

Two practical consequences follow. First, any buyer who knows the rules will deduct the cost of the repairs, plus their risk, from the price. Second, open fines and city charges do not vanish at closing; unpaid amounts on the lien certificate are typically paid from your proceeds. Under the same section of the code, unresolved violations can lead to criminal fines of up to $500 per offense or civil fines of up to $500 for each day a violation continues, so it pays to resolve the situation rather than wait.

In our experience working with Baltimore sellers, the surprise is rarely the violation itself. It is the stack of smaller city charges, like old water bills and boarding fees, that show up on the lien certificate a week before settlement. Order it early.

What Must You Disclose When Selling in Maryland?

Maryland gives sellers of one-to-four unit homes two choices under Real Property § 10-702: complete a disclosure statement describing the condition of the house, or sign a disclaimer statement and sell as-is. Both use the state's Residential Property Disclosure and Disclaimer Statement. The statute exempts some transfers, so ask your attorney whether yours qualifies.

Selling as-is does not mean saying nothing. Even with a disclaimer, you must disclose latent defects you actually know about: material problems a careful visual inspection would not reveal, such as a hidden foundation crack or a known sewer line failure. Combine that with the Baltimore VBN disclosure rule and the safest approach is simple. Hand the buyer copies of every notice you have received.

What Are Your Options for Selling a House With Code Violations?

Sellers with code violations in Baltimore generally have four realistic paths. The right one depends on how serious the violations are, how much cash you can put in, and how much time you have.

A cash buyer like us will usually offer less than a repaired house would bring on the open market, because we take on the repairs, the permits and the risk of unknown problems behind the walls. If your violations are a few hundred dollars of work and the house is otherwise in good shape, fix them and list with a good local agent. A cash sale makes the most sense when the repairs are large, the house carries a VBN, or you need a certain closing date.

What Happens If a Vacant Baltimore House Is Left Unresolved?

Baltimore can force the sale of a vacant nuisance property through a court process called receivership. DHCD explains that before taking legal action it issues violation notices and citations and orders cleaning and boarding. If the owner does not respond, DHCD can file a receivership case, and the court can appoint a receiver to sell the property to a buyer who will rehab it. The city also uses a newer judicial in rem foreclosure tool for some vacant properties with VBNs.

Receivership is not the end of the road, but it takes control away from you. The sale happens on the court's timeline, at an auction price, and costs and taxes come out first. In our experience, owners who sell or start repairs before a case is filed almost always come out ahead.

How Much Does It Cost to Sell a House With Violations in Baltimore?

Selling costs in Baltimore start with transfer taxes. The city charges a 1.5% transfer tax on the sale price, according to the Bureau of Revenue Collections, and Maryland charges a separate state transfer tax, plus a recordation tax. Who pays each one is negotiated in the contract. Add any unpaid city charges on the lien certificate, and, if you list, agent commission and repair costs.

For a sale price of $150,000, the city transfer tax alone is $2,250. Ask your settlement company for a written net sheet before you choose a path, so you are comparing real numbers. When we make an offer, we cover closing costs and show our math, including the repair estimate behind it.
