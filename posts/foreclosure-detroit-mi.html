Facing Foreclosure in Detroit, MI: Your 2026 Options

By Matt Vorwerk September 21, 2026 9 min read

If you've missed a few mortgage payments on a house in Detroit, the letters start arriving fast — and they're written in a language nobody teaches you. "Notice of default." "Foreclosure by advertisement." "Sheriff's sale." "Redemption period." It's a lot, especially when you're already dealing with whatever caused the missed payments in the first place.

Here's the part most people don't realize: Michigan's process gives you more time and more decision points than almost anyone expects. You are not one letter away from losing the house. This guide walks through what actually happens, on what timeline, and what your realistic choices are at each stage — whether your home is in Rosedale Park, East English Village, Bagley, Corktown, or Jefferson-Chalmers.

How Foreclosure Actually Works in Michigan

Michigan is primarily a non-judicial foreclosure state. That means most lenders never file a lawsuit against you. Instead they use a process called foreclosure by advertisement, authorized under MCL 600.3204, where the mortgage is foreclosed through published legal notices and a sheriff's sale rather than a courtroom.

Non-judicial sounds scarier than it is. It's faster than the judicial process used in states like Ohio or Florida, but Michigan balances that speed with something those states don't offer: a statutory redemption period after the sale, during which you still own and can still live in the home. More on that below, because it's the single most misunderstood part of the process.

Stage 1: Default and the pre-foreclosure notice

Most mortgage servicers begin formal collection after you're roughly 30 to 45 days behind, and under federal servicing rules they generally can't start the foreclosure process until the loan is more than 120 days delinquent. That first four months is quiet from a legal standpoint but loud from a mail standpoint.

Before a foreclosure sale of a property claimed as a principal residence, Michigan law (MCL 600.3205a) requires the foreclosing party to send you a written notice with a designated contact person. That notice must tell you that within 14 days you may request a meeting to discuss modifying the loan, and it must include a list of HUD-approved or MSHDA-approved housing counselors. If you contact a counselor within that window, MCL 600.3205b and 600.3205c trigger obligations on the lender's side around evaluating you for a modification.

Don't throw that letter away. The 14-day counselor window is the cheapest, lowest-risk move available to you, and the counseling itself is free. Even if you're certain you want to sell, a counselor can tell you exactly where you stand — which makes every other decision easier.

Stage 2: Publication and posting

If the default isn't cured, the lender publishes a notice of foreclosure sale. Under MCL 600.3208, that notice must run once a week for four successive weeks in a newspaper in the county where the property sits — Wayne County for Detroit proper. Within 15 days after the first publication, a copy has to be posted in a conspicuous place on the property itself.

That posted notice is often the moment it becomes real for homeowners, and it's also the moment the phone starts ringing, because published foreclosure notices are public and investors watch them closely. You are under no obligation to talk to any of those callers.

Stage 3: The sheriff's sale

After the fourth week of publication, the property is sold at a sheriff's sale — in Wayne County, these are typically held on a regular weekly schedule. The lender usually bids the amount it's owed; sometimes a third party bids more. Either way, the winning bidder receives a sheriff's deed, and that deed does not become operative until the redemption period expires.

Stage 4: The redemption period — you still own the home

This is the piece almost everyone gets wrong. Under MCL 600.3240, Michigan gives you a redemption period after the sheriff's sale. For residential property of four units or fewer, the period is generally six months when the amount claimed due at the date of the foreclosure notice is more than two-thirds of the original loan amount. If you've paid the balance down below that two-thirds mark, the redemption period is longer — twelve months in many cases. Certain conditions, including a property that has been abandoned, can shorten it.

During redemption you retain possession, you can live in the house, and you can sell it. Redeeming means paying the sale price plus statutory interest and allowable costs. Most homeowners can't write that check — but most homeowners also don't realize they can sell the property during redemption and use the proceeds to pay off the winning bid, keeping whatever equity is left instead of handing it to the bidder.

The math that matters: if your Detroit home is worth more than what's owed plus sale costs, that difference is yours — but only if you act before the redemption period closes. After it expires, the equity goes to the sheriff's-deed holder. Every month of delay narrows your options.

A Separate Risk: Wayne County Property Tax Foreclosure

Mortgage foreclosure isn't the only clock running in Detroit. Delinquent property taxes trigger an entirely different process under Michigan's General Property Tax Act (MCL 211.78 et seq.), run by the Wayne County Treasurer rather than your lender. It moves on its own multi-year schedule and ends in a county tax foreclosure regardless of who holds the mortgage.

If you're behind on both, treat the tax delinquency as a separate problem with its own deadlines. The Wayne County Treasurer's office offers payment plans, and the city and county have run poverty tax exemption and interest-reduction programs that meaningfully change the numbers for qualifying owners. It's worth a phone call before you assume the worst.

Five Realistic Ways Out

1. Reinstate or modify the loan

If the hardship that caused the default is over — you're back to work, the medical bills are behind you — reinstatement (paying the arrears plus fees) or a loan modification may put you back on track. Modifications can extend the term, capitalize the arrears, or reduce the rate. This is the best outcome when the underlying income is there. It is not realistic if the monthly payment was never affordable to begin with.

2. Forbearance or a repayment plan

A short-term forbearance pauses or reduces payments for a set period, with the missed amount repaid later. It buys breathing room for a temporary disruption. Get the terms in writing and make sure you understand how the paused payments come back — some plans require a lump sum at the end, which can leave you worse off.

3. List the home on the market

If you have equity and enough runway before the sale, listing with a Detroit agent generally produces the highest top-line price. Be honest with yourself about the timeline: prep, marketing, showings, an accepted offer, an appraisal, and a buyer's financing contingency all take weeks. Budget 5–6% in commissions and another 1–2% in closing costs, plus the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities you're paying every month it's on the market. If the house needs work, buyers using FHA or conventional financing may not be able to close on it at all.

4. Short sale

If you owe more than the home is worth, a short sale lets you sell with the lender's approval for less than the balance. It protects your credit better than a completed foreclosure and sometimes comes with relocation assistance. The tradeoff is time and uncertainty — lender approval can take 60 to 120 days, and it isn't guaranteed.

5. Sell as-is to a cash buyer

A direct cash sale skips repairs, showings, appraisals, and financing contingencies. You typically get an offer within 24 to 48 hours and pick the closing date, which matters enormously when a sheriff's sale or redemption deadline is on the calendar. The honest tradeoff: a cash offer is below retail, generally in the range of 70–85% of what the home would bring fully renovated on the MLS, because the buyer absorbs the repair cost, holding cost, and risk. That's the right trade when speed and certainty are worth more than maximum price — and the wrong one when you have six months, a solid house, and the patience to list it. What to Do This Week

  • Open every letter and build a timeline. Write down the date of your first missed payment, the date of any MCL 600.3205a notice, and any publication or sale date you've been given. Most panic comes from not knowing where you are.
  • Call a HUD- or MSHDA-approved housing counselor. It's free and it's the fastest way to get an accurate read on modification eligibility.
  • Pull a realistic value on the house. Not Zillow — an agent's comparative market analysis or an appraisal. You cannot make a good decision without knowing whether you have equity.
  • Check your Wayne County tax status separately. Two clocks, two sets of deadlines.
  • Talk to a Michigan attorney if anything looks irregular. Defects in notice, publication, or posting are real and occasionally matter. Nothing in this article is legal advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my Detroit house after the sheriff's sale?

Yes. During the redemption period under MCL 600.3240 you still own the property and can sell it. The sale has to close before redemption expires, and the proceeds pay off the winning bid plus interest and allowable costs — you keep what's left. This is the most commonly missed opportunity in Michigan foreclosure, and it's why the redemption window is worth treating as a deadline rather than a grace period.

How long does foreclosure take in Michigan from the first missed payment?

Roughly nine to fifteen months for a typical owner-occupied case: about 120 days of delinquency before the process can formally begin, the MCL 600.3205a notice and 14-day counselor window, four weeks of published notice, the sheriff's sale, and then the six-month (sometimes twelve-month) redemption period. Timelines vary with the servicer, the county calendar, and your own response.

Do I have to fill out a seller's disclosure if I'm selling during foreclosure?

Usually yes. Michigan's Seller Disclosure Act (MCL 565.951 et seq.) requires a Seller's Disclosure Statement for most residential sales of one to four units. The exemptions in MCL 565.953 cover things like transfers at a foreclosure sale, deeds in lieu of foreclosure, and transfers by a nonoccupant fiduciary administering an estate — but an owner selling their own home before the sale is generally still on the hook. Disclose what you know; an as-is sale doesn't erase that duty.

Will a cash buyer take a Detroit house that needs major work?

That's usually the point. Roof, foundation, fire damage, code violations, a house full of belongings, a tenant still in place — those are the conditions that make traditional financing impossible and cash sales practical. You don't need to clean it out, repair anything, or stage it. If the numbers don't work for both sides, we'll say so and point you somewhere better.

The Bottom Line

Foreclosure in Michigan is a process with defined stages and real off-ramps at each one. The homeowners who come out of it in the best shape are almost never the ones with the most money — they're the ones who opened the mail, figured out which week they were in, and made a decision while they still had choices. If you're somewhere in that timeline right now, you likely have more room than you think.
