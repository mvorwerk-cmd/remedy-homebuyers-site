Inherited a House in Atlanta? A Step-by-Step Guide to Georgia Probate Sales

By Matt Vorwerk June 8, 2026 8 min read

Inheriting a house in Atlanta is rarely as simple as being handed a set of keys. There is usually a will to locate, a probate court to deal with, repairs nobody planned for, sometimes siblings who live in different states and have different ideas, and a tax code that feels designed to trip you up. If a loved one recently passed and left you a home in the metro area, you are likely grieving and problem-solving at the same time — and that is a hard place to be.

This guide walks through what actually happens, step by step, when you inherit a property in Fulton, DeKalb, Cobb, or one of the surrounding counties and decide to sell. It is written to help you understand your options in plain English, even if you never call us. Georgia's probate system has a few features — like "year's support" and a "no administration necessary" shortcut — that can save heirs real time and money once you know they exist.

Step 1: Find Out Whether the House Has to Go Through Probate

Probate is the court process of transferring a deceased person's assets to their heirs. Whether the Atlanta home you inherited has to go through it depends entirely on how the previous owner held title:

  • Living trust: If the home was titled in a properly funded revocable living trust, it usually avoids probate. The successor trustee can sell the property directly.
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship: Title passes automatically to the surviving co-owner, with no probate required.
  • Sole ownership, with or without a will: Probate is generally required. Georgia offers both "common form" probate (faster, but it can be challenged for up to four years) and "solemn form" probate (slower, but final once granted).

Georgia's probate rules live in Title 53 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated, the Revised Probate Code. Each county runs its own Probate Court — in Atlanta, most estates are handled by the Fulton County Probate Court on Pryor Street downtown, or the DeKalb County Probate Court if the home sits on the east side of the city.

Step 2: Ask Whether You Can Skip Full Administration

This is the step most heirs do not know about, and it can save months. Georgia lets families avoid a full, formal estate administration in two common situations.

No Administration Necessary

If the decedent's debts have all been paid (or the creditors agree), and all the heirs agree on how to divide the estate, you can file a Petition for Order Declaring No Administration Necessary. There is no estate-size cap on this option — what matters is that the debts are handled and the heirs are on the same page. When the court grants it, you get an order confirming who owns the property, and you can move to sell without appointing a full administrator.

Year's Support

Georgia's "year's support" (O.C.G.A. § 53-3-1) is one of the strongest spousal protections in the country. A surviving spouse, and any minor children, can petition the probate court to set aside property — including the home — to support them for the year following the death. Year's support takes priority over almost every creditor claim, and there is no statutory dollar cap. The petition generally has to be filed within two years of the date of death. If year's support absorbs the whole estate, no further administration may be needed at all.

Reality check: Even a "simple" Georgia estate rarely closes in under six months. The personal representative must publish a notice to creditors, and creditors then get three months from the end of that publication to bring a claim (O.C.G.A. § 53-7-41). Plan for roughly 6 to 12 months for a clean estate, and longer if the will is contested.

Step 3: Understand the Stepped-Up Tax Basis (Good News for Once)

Here is a rare bit of good news in tax law. When you inherit real estate, your cost basis "steps up" to the home's fair market value on the date of the owner's death. Say your father bought a bungalow in Grant Park in 1985 for $48,000, and it was worth $410,000 when he passed. Your basis is $410,000 — not $48,000.

Why it matters: if you sell near that date-of-death value, you will likely owe little or no capital gains tax. Decades of appreciation are effectively wiped clean. Sell a year later for $425,000, and you are taxed only on the $15,000 of gain since you inherited, not the full run-up since 1985. On top of that, Georgia charges no state estate tax and no inheritance tax, so the federal step-up is usually the whole story for most families.

Get a qualified appraisal or a detailed broker's opinion of value dated as close to the date of death as possible, and keep it in the estate file. Your CPA will need it the year you sell.

Step 4: Decide What to Actually Do With the House

Atlanta heirs generally land in one of four camps. None of them is wrong — the right answer depends on your situation, not a rulebook.

Option A: Move into it

If the house fits your life, this is the simplest path. Just budget for the carrying costs — property taxes, insurance, and upkeep — and confirm there are no liens or back taxes attached to the home before you settle in.

Option B: Rent it out

Atlanta's rental market is strong, especially for single-family homes in areas like East Atlanta, Kirkwood, and Cascade Heights. But being a landlord is a real job — repairs, screening tenants, vacancies, and the occasional eviction — and managing it from out of state is harder still. A rough rule: if the house needs more than about $15,000 of work to be rent-ready, renting is usually not the fastest route to cash.

Option C: Fix it up and sell on the open market

This path produces the highest price on paper — but "on paper" is carrying a lot of weight. Expect to spend roughly $10,000 to $60,000 on repairs and updates, 30 to 90 days on market, 5 to 6% in agent commissions, 1 to 2% in closing costs, and monthly holding costs the whole time. For an heir who lives out of the area or works full time, the logistics can be exhausting.

Option D: Sell as-is to a cash buyer

A direct cash sale skips the repairs, the listings, the showings, and the financing contingencies. You typically get an offer within 24 to 48 hours, choose your closing date, and walk away with a check. The honest tradeoff is that a cash offer is below retail — generally 70 to 85% of what the home would fetch fully renovated on the open market — because the buyer is taking on the repairs, the risk, and the holding costs. Whether that tradeoff is worth it comes down to how much time, money, and stress you want to spend on the alternative.

Step 5: Handle the Stuff Inside the House

Most heirs underestimate this part. A lifetime of belongings can take days or weeks to sort through, and you will want to preserve anything that could matter if the will is ever questioned. A few practical tips:

  • Take dated photos or a quick phone video of every room before touching anything.
  • Check every drawer, closet, coat pocket, and book for cash, jewelry, deeds, and insurance papers before donating or discarding.
  • Estate sale companies around Atlanta typically take 25 to 40% of gross proceeds — usually worth it if there are 50 or more items of real value.
  • A full cleanout often runs $1,500 to $4,500 depending on volume. A good cash buyer will frequently take the house with the contents still inside, saving you that step entirely.

Step 6: Navigate the Family Dynamics

If you are not the only heir, this is often harder than every other step combined. Everyone has a different financial situation, a different emotional attachment, and a different idea of what the home is worth. A few ground rules tend to keep families intact:

  • Get one independent valuation everyone agrees to upfront. Don't argue about the price — agree on which appraiser to use, then accept the number they produce.
  • Decide early whether you are selling or keeping. Mixed goals almost always end in a forced sale anyway, usually at a worse price after months of friction.
  • Put the agreement in writing, even informally. Email counts. Verbal promises between siblings tend to evaporate under stress.
  • If one heir wants to buy out the others, use that same independent valuation and add a small premium (5 to 10%) for the convenience of avoiding a market sale.

How Long Does the Whole Process Take?

Best case (trust or no-administration order, no repairs, cash sale): about 2 to 4 weeks from agreement to closing.

Typical case (modest probate, some cleanup, cash sale): 1 to 3 months.

Full administration plus an open-market sale: 9 to 18 months or more.

The biggest variable is the court. If the estate has to go through full administration, you are waiting on the probate court — not the housing market — for much of that timeline, no matter which sale path you eventually choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to go through probate to sell an inherited house in Atlanta?

Not always. If the home was in a living trust, in joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or passed by beneficiary deed, it can transfer without probate. If it was solely owned, you will usually need either a probate proceeding or — when all heirs agree and the debts are handled — a Petition for Order Declaring No Administration Necessary in the county probate court.

How long does probate take in Fulton County?

A straightforward estate in Fulton, DeKalb, or the surrounding counties generally runs about 6 to 12 months. The creditor claim period alone lasts three months after the notice to creditors finishes publishing, so even clean estates rarely wrap up in under six months.

Will I owe capital gains tax if I sell an inherited Atlanta home?

Usually very little. Your basis steps up to the home's value on the date of death, so selling near that value means you owe tax only on any gain since then. Georgia also charges no state estate or inheritance tax. Keep a date-of-death valuation for your CPA.

Can I sell the house before probate is finished?

Often, yes. You can accept an offer during probate, and an experienced buyer can work alongside the court timeline so the closing happens once the personal representative has authority to sign. A cash buyer who understands probate can hold the agreement open while the process runs.
