How to Sell a Distressed Property in San Antonio Without a Realtor

By Matt Vorwerk July 27, 2026 9 min read

If you own a run-down, damaged, or just plain tired property in San Antonio, the idea of listing it with an agent can feel like more than you signed up for. Repairs before it will even show well. Weeks of strangers walking through. Commissions that eat into whatever equity you have left. For a lot of owners in that spot, selling without a realtor — either on your own or directly to a cash buyer — is worth a serious look.

This guide walks through how to sell a distressed San Antonio house without an agent: what "distressed" really means to a buyer, what Texas law requires you to disclose, how to price a property that needs work, and the realistic paths you have. It's written to help you make a good decision, even if that decision has nothing to do with us.

What Counts as a "Distressed" Property?

"Distressed" is a broad word, and it doesn't only mean a house falling apart. In practice, a property is distressed when its condition or the owner's situation makes a normal retail sale difficult. That covers a wide range:

  • Physical condition: foundation movement (common in San Antonio's clay soils), roof damage, fire or water damage, outdated systems, or a home that hasn't been updated in decades.
  • Financial pressure: falling behind on the mortgage, mounting property taxes, or a lien that needs to be cleared.
  • Life circumstances: an inherited home you can't maintain, a divorce, a job relocation, or a rental you're tired of managing.
  • Code issues: open violations from the City of San Antonio's Development Services Department that a traditional buyer's lender won't overlook.

Why the label matters: most conventional buyers use financing, and lenders won't approve a mortgage on a home with major defects. That quietly removes a big slice of the buyer pool and pushes distressed homes toward cash buyers and investors — which shapes every decision below.

Step 1: Know What Texas Requires You to Disclose

Selling without an agent doesn't mean selling without rules. Under Texas Property Code § 5.008, a seller of residential property with not more than one dwelling unit generally must give the buyer a written Seller's Disclosure Notice describing the property's known condition — including defects in the roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, and any known flooding.

The statute also lists exemptions in § 5.008(e). Transfers under a court order, sales by an estate's executor or administrator, transfers to a spouse or direct descendants, and foreclosure-related transfers are among the situations that can be exempt from the notice requirement. If your sale falls into one of those buckets — say you inherited the home and are selling through the estate — you may not be legally required to deliver the standard notice.

Our honest take: even when you're exempt, telling the truth about the property protects you. "As-is" limits your obligation to repair — it does not give you permission to hide a known defect. Disclosing upfront is both the right thing to do and the best defense against a dispute after closing. When in doubt, ask a Texas real estate attorney whether your specific sale is exempt.

Step 2: Get Honest About the Numbers

The single biggest mistake we see with for-sale-by-owner distressed homes is pricing off the wrong comps. It's tempting to look at the beautifully renovated house down the street that sold for top dollar and assume yours is worth almost that much. It isn't — and pretending otherwise just means months of no offers.

A cleaner way to think about value on a distressed property is the same math a cash buyer uses:

  • After-Repair Value (ARV): what the home would be worth fully fixed up, based on recently sold, renovated comps in your specific neighborhood.
  • Minus repair costs: an honest estimate of what it takes to get there. On older San Antonio homes, foundation work alone can run $8,000 to $25,000-plus.
  • Minus selling and holding costs: everything it takes to carry and resell the home.
  • Minus a fair margin for whoever takes on that risk and work.

Whatever's left is roughly what a cash buyer can pay. If you sell it yourself to an owner-occupant instead, you might capture some of that repair-and-margin spread — but only if you're willing to do the repairs, wait out the market, and manage the process. That's the real trade-off, and it's worth being clear-eyed about before you list.

Step 3: Understand Your Neighborhood

San Antonio isn't one market — it's dozens. A distressed bungalow in a hot near-downtown pocket sells very differently from a worn rental on the far edge of town. A few examples of how location changes the calculus:

  • Dignowity Hill and Government Hill: these historic East Side neighborhoods have seen strong investor and renovator demand. A fixer here can attract multiple cash buyers who want the bones and the location. Note that historic-district rules can affect what a renovator is allowed to change.
  • Beacon Hill and Monte Vista's edges: older homes near the Central district draw buyers who value character and walkability, even when the house needs work.
  • The Southside and South San Antonio: more affordable, steady rental demand, and a common landing spot for investors buying to hold rather than flip.
  • Stone Oak and the far North: newer housing stock, so "distressed" here is usually about a life event or deferred maintenance rather than an old structure — and buyer financing is more likely to be in play.

Knowing which of these describes your property tells you who your likely buyer is, how quickly it may move, and whether financed buyers are even realistic.

Step 4: Choose Your Path

Selling without a realtor gives you a few genuinely different routes. None is automatically best — it depends on how much time, money, and stress you want to spend.

Option A: True FSBO to a retail buyer

You market the home yourself, handle showings, negotiate, and coordinate closing with a title company. You save the listing-side commission, but you'll do real work and, on a distressed home, you're fishing in a smaller pond because financed buyers usually can't qualify for a house that needs major work. Best when the home is only lightly distressed and you have time.

Option B: Sell to a local investor or flipper

Investors buy as-is and close on cash, but their offers reflect the repair-and-margin math above. The upside is speed and certainty; the trade-off is a below-retail price. Verify you're dealing with a buyer who actually closes — not a wholesaler who ties up your house under contract and then shops it around.

Option C: Sell to a direct cash buyer like us

A reputable cash buyer gives you a written offer, buys the home in its current condition, pays typical closing costs, and lets you pick the closing date — often in as few as 7 to 21 days. You skip repairs, showings, and financing contingencies. The offer is below retail, and we'll always show you the math so you can compare it honestly against listing.

Option D: Fix, then list yourself

If the repairs are modest and you have the cash and patience, doing the cheap, high-impact work (paint, cleanup, landscaping, minor repairs) and then selling FSBO can net you the most. Skip the big renovations unless real comps justify them — a $35,000 kitchen rarely returns $35,000.

Step 5: Close It Cleanly

In Texas, home sales close through a title company, not an attorney's office, which makes a no-agent sale very manageable. Whichever path you choose:

  • Use a reputable San Antonio title company to run title, clear any liens, and handle the paperwork and funds.
  • Get every agreement in writing — price, closing date, who pays what, and what stays with the house.
  • Ask a cash buyer for proof of funds before you sign anything.
  • If there are back taxes, code liens, or probate involved, tell the title company early so nothing derails closing at the last minute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really sell a house in San Antonio without a realtor?

Yes. Texas doesn't require you to use a real estate agent to sell your home. You can sell for-sale-by-owner to a retail buyer, or sell directly to an investor or cash buyer. Because Texas closings run through a title company, the paperwork and funds are handled by a neutral third party, which keeps a no-agent sale straightforward.

Do I have to make repairs before selling a distressed property?

No. You can sell a property strictly as-is, meaning the buyer accepts the home in its current condition and you make no repairs. Selling as-is limits your obligation to fix things, but under Texas Property Code § 5.008 it does not remove your duty to disclose known defects when the notice applies to your sale.

How fast can I sell a distressed San Antonio home for cash?

With a direct cash buyer, a written offer often comes within 24 to 48 hours, and closing can happen in as few as 7 to 21 days once title is clear. The main variables are clearing any liens, resolving code violations, and — if the property was inherited — the estate's status.

Will I get less money selling without an agent?

Not necessarily. You save the listing commission, but a distressed home sold to a cash buyer will price below full retail because that buyer absorbs the repairs, risk, and holding costs. The honest way to decide is to compare a real cash offer against your likely net after repairs, commissions, and months of holding costs on the open market.
